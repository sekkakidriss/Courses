IS-LM and AD-AS

Two pairs of curves that every macroeconomist learns first. IS-LM shows how spending and interest rates settle in the short run. AD-AS adds prices, and shows how the economy drifts back to normal over time.

A small model economy. The solid lines are the IS and LM curves. Where they cross, both the goods market and the money market are in balance. Move the slider.

The goods market

Output adjusts until what is produced equals what people want to buy.

The idea

In the short run, firms produce what they can sell. So total output is driven by total demand for goods, which has three parts: what households spend (consumption), what firms spend on machines and buildings (investment), and what the government spends.

The key twist is that spending creates income. If the government pays for a new road, the builders earn wages. They spend part of those wages in shops, whose owners spend part of their extra income, and so on. One unit of new spending ends up raising output by more than one unit. This is the multiplier.

The formulas

Demand \(Z\) is consumption plus investment plus government spending. Consumption depends on disposable income, income \(Y\) minus taxes \(T\):

\[ Z = C + I + G, \qquad C = c_0 + c_1\,(Y - T). \]

Here \(c_0\) is spending that does not depend on income, and \(c_1\) is the marginal propensity to consume: the share of each extra unit of income that is spent (between 0 and 1). Equilibrium means output equals demand, \(Y = Z\). Solving:

\[ Y = \frac{1}{1 - c_1}\,\big(c_0 - c_1 T + I + G\big). \]

The factor \(1/(1 - c_1)\) is the multiplier.

Try it

This chart is called the Keynesian cross. The amber line is demand at each level of output. The grey diagonal is where output equals demand. Equilibrium is where they cross. Investment is held at 200 for now.

Worked example

With \(c_1 = 0.6\), the multiplier is \(1/(1 - 0.6) = 2.5\). If government spending rises by 40, output rises by \(2.5 \times 40 = 100\). The rounds of spending are 40, then \(0.6 \times 40 = 24\), then 14.4, and so on, adding up to 100.

Check yourself

If households spend 80% of each extra unit of income, what is the multiplier?
1 / (1 - 0.8) = 5.

The IS curve

Higher interest rates mean less investment, and therefore less output.

The idea

Firms borrow to invest. When the interest rate is high, borrowing is expensive and fewer projects are worth doing, so investment falls. Through the multiplier, output falls by more than investment.

For each interest rate there is one level of output at which the goods market balances. Joining these points gives a downward-sloping line called the IS curve (IS stands for Investment equals Saving, another way to say the goods market is in equilibrium).

The formula

Let investment fall with the interest rate \(i\) (in percent): \(I = I_0 - d\,i\). Substituting into the goods market equilibrium:

\[ Y = \frac{1}{1 - c_1}\,\big(c_0 - c_1 T + I_0 + G - d\,i\big). \]

A change in \(i\) moves the economy along the curve. A change in \(G\), \(T\) or confidence (\(c_0\), \(I_0\)) shifts the whole curve.

Try it

Worked example

With \(c_0 = 200\), \(c_1 = 0.6\), \(T = 200\), \(I_0 = 300\), \(d = 20\) and \(G = 320\), the IS curve is \(Y = (700 - 20\,i)/0.4 = 1750 - 50\,i\). At \(i = 5\%\), output is 1500. Raising the rate to 7% lowers it to 1400.

Check yourself

Which of these shifts the IS curve to the right?
More government spending raises demand at every interest rate. A higher interest rate only moves along the curve; higher taxes and lower confidence shift it left.

The LM curve

The money market, and where the interest rate comes from.

The idea

People hold money to pay for things. The more the economy produces and sells, the more money people need for transactions. But holding money has a cost: money in your pocket earns no interest, while a bond does. So when interest rates are high, people hold less money.

Suppose the central bank fixes the amount of money. If output rises, people want more money for transactions. With the supply fixed, the interest rate has to rise until people are content holding the money that exists. So higher output goes with a higher interest rate. This upward-sloping line is the LM curve (L for liquidity, the demand for money, and M for the money supply).

The formula

Real money demand (money divided by the price level \(P\)) rises with output and falls with the interest rate. In equilibrium it equals the real money supply \(M/P\):

\[ \frac{M}{P} = k\,Y - h\,i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i = \frac{1}{h}\left(k\,Y - \frac{M}{P}\right). \]

More money (\(M\) up) or lower prices (\(P\) down) shift the LM curve down: the same output now goes with a lower interest rate.

Try it

Left: the money market, with demand for money at the chosen output and the fixed supply. Right: the LM curve that results.

A modern note: most central banks today set an interest rate directly and supply whatever money is demanded at that rate. In that view the LM curve is simply a horizontal line at the chosen rate. Block 4 lets you switch between the two views.

Worked example

With \(k = 0.5\), \(h = 50\), \(M = 500\) and \(P = 1\), the LM curve is \(i = (0.5\,Y - 500)/50 = 0.01\,Y - 10\). At \(Y = 1500\), \(i = 5\%\). If output rises by 200, money demand rises by 100, and the rate must rise 2 points to 7% to choke off that extra demand.

Check yourself

The central bank increases the money supply. What happens to the LM curve?
With more money available, people hold it willingly only at a lower interest rate, for any given level of output.

IS-LM and policy

Both markets in balance at once, and what fiscal and monetary policy do.

The idea

The IS curve lists combinations of output and interest rate where the goods market balances. The LM curve does the same for the money market. Only one point is on both: the short-run equilibrium.

Fiscal policy (government spending and taxes) shifts IS. Monetary policy (the money supply or the policy rate) shifts LM. One effect deserves attention: crowding out. When the government spends more, output rises, money demand rises, and if the money supply is fixed the interest rate goes up. That higher rate reduces private investment, so output rises by less than the simple multiplier promised.

The formula

Solving the IS and LM equations together, with \(A = c_0 - c_1 T + I_0 + G\):

\[ Y = \frac{A + d\,\dfrac{M}{P h}}{(1 - c_1) + \dfrac{d\,k}{h}}. \]

The extra term \(dk/h\) in the denominator is crowding out: it makes the fiscal multiplier smaller than \(1/(1 - c_1)\).

Try it

Dashed lines show the starting point. Change policy and compare.

IS curveLM curveStarting curves

Raise \(G\) by 60 in each mode. With a fixed money supply, output rises 100. With the rate held at 5%, it rises the full 150, because the central bank supplies the extra money and nothing is crowded out.

Worked example

Starting at \(Y = 1500\), \(i = 5\%\), government spending rises from 320 to 380. The new equilibrium is \(Y = (760 + 200)/0.6 = 1600\) and \(i = 6\%\). The simple multiplier promised \(2.5 \times 60 = 150\); the higher rate cut investment by 20, which through the multiplier removed 50 of output.

Check yourself

A fiscal expansion has the largest effect on output when...
If the rate does not rise, investment is not crowded out, and the full multiplier applies.

Aggregate demand

Letting prices change: from IS-LM to the AD curve.

The idea

So far prices were fixed. Now ask: what if the price level \(P\) is higher? The same amount of money buys less, so the real money supply \(M/P\) is smaller. In the money market this pushes the interest rate up, which lowers investment and output.

So a higher price level goes with lower demand for output. Plotting, for each price level, the output that IS-LM delivers gives the AD (Aggregate Demand) curve. It slopes down, not because each good gets more expensive relative to others, but because of what prices do to the real money supply and interest rates.

The formula

The AD curve is the IS-LM solution written as a function of \(P\):

\[ Y^{AD}(P) = \frac{A + d\,\dfrac{M}{P h}}{(1 - c_1) + \dfrac{d\,k}{h}}. \]

A change in \(P\) moves along AD. Fiscal or monetary policy shifts it.

Try it

Change the price level. On top, the LM curve moves. Below, the result traces the AD curve.

Worked example

If \(P\) rises from 1 to 1.25, the real money supply falls from 500 to 400. Output becomes \((700 + 20 \times 400/50)/0.6 = (700 + 160)/0.6 \approx 1433\), down from 1500, and the interest rate rises to about 6.3%.

Check yourself

Why does the AD curve slope down?
In this model the channel runs through real money balances and the interest rate.

Aggregate supply

How prices respond when output is above or below normal.

The idea

When firms produce more, they hire more and unemployment falls. Workers then have more bargaining power and wages rise. Firms set prices as a markup over their costs, so higher wages mean higher prices. Output above normal therefore goes with prices above what people expected.

Expectations matter because wages are usually set in advance, based on the prices workers expect. If everyone expects higher prices, wages are set higher, and the whole curve shifts up.

There is one level of output at which prices turn out exactly as expected: natural output \(Y_n\), the output produced when unemployment is at its natural rate. It is the same idea as potential output in the Okun's law lecture.

The formula

A simple version of the AS (Aggregate Supply) relation, with expected price level \(P^e\):

\[ P = P^e \left(1 + \mu\,\frac{Y - Y_n}{Y_n}\right). \]

When \(Y = Y_n\), \(P = P^e\). Each 1% of output above \(Y_n\) pushes prices about \(\mu\) percent above expectations.

Try it

Worked example

With \(Y_n = 1500\), \(\mu = 1\) and \(P^e = 1\), producing 1560 (4% above natural output) gives \(P = 1 \times (1 + 0.04) = 1.04\): prices end up 4% higher than people expected.

Check yourself

Workers start expecting higher prices. What happens to the AS curve?
Higher expected prices lead to higher wage settlements, so firms charge higher prices at every level of output.

Shocks and adjustment

The short run, the medium run, and the path between them.

The idea

Put AD and AS together. Where they cross, we get output and the price level. Now hit the economy with a shock. In the short run, output can move away from natural output. But if prices turn out different from what people expected, they revise their expectations. Each year the AS curve shifts, until output is back at \(Y_n\) and expectations are right again. That end point is the medium run.

The model assumes people expect this year's prices to equal last year's: \(P^e_t = P_{t-1}\). This is the same adaptive expectations idea as in the Phillips curve lecture.

Worked example

After the money supply rises from 500 to 600, the medium run has \(Y = 1500\) and \(i = 5\%\) again, so from the LM equation \(5 = (750 - 600/P)/50\), which gives \(P = 1.2\): prices rose exactly 20%, like the money supply.

Check yourself

In the medium run of this model, what does a permanent fiscal expansion change?
Output returns to \(Y_n\), but with a higher interest rate, so private investment is lower and government spending higher.

Limits

LimitWhat it means
No explicit futureSpending depends on current income only. Households and firms do not look ahead, which is exactly what DSGE models add.
Adaptive expectationsPeople keep making the same forecasting mistake for years. Later models use anchored or rational expectations.
The zero lower boundInterest rates cannot go much below zero. In a deep recession the central bank cannot cut further and LM becomes flat at zero, a liquidity trap. Then fiscal policy matters more.
Closed economyNo trade or exchange rates. Open-economy versions (Mundell-Fleming) are needed for small open economies, especially with a fixed exchange rate.
Linear and staticUseful for direction and intuition, not for precise numbers.

Check yourself

In a liquidity trap with the interest rate at zero, which policy can still raise output?
With LM flat at zero, more money cannot lower rates further, but shifting IS through fiscal policy still raises output, with no crowding out.
