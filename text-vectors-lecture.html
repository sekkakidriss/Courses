Removing the frequent and the empty

Stopwords

Words that appear almost everywhere carry almost no information about which document you are holding. Removing them shortens vectors and speeds everything up.

Stoplist in use
A stoplist is a modelling choice, not a fact. Removing negations destroys sentiment analysis, and removing function words destroys authorship attribution, where those words are the signal. TF-IDF in Part 04 down-weights them automatically, which is usually the better instrument.

Part 03

N-grams: buying back a little word order

A bag of words forgets that not good and good differ. Sliding a window of length n over the sequence keeps local order, at a price you can watch below.

The sliding window
The n-grams it produces
Cost of the choice, whole corpus
Why n stays small
A sequence of length L yields L - n + 1 n-grams. The number of possible n-grams is |V|n, so the feature space explodes while each individual feature gets rarer. With a vocabulary of 20000 words, bigrams already allow 400 million columns, of which a corpus will ever show a tiny fraction. In practice n is 1 to 3 for words and 3 to 5 for characters, where character n-grams are the standard trick for misspellings, morphology rich languages and language identification.
Part 04

Documents as vectors

Count how often each vocabulary entry occurs in a document and you have a row of numbers. Do it for every document and you have a matrix. Everything a classical text model does is linear algebra on that matrix.

Bag of words

The document term matrix

Document under the cursor
What the matrix throws away
The row for a document is the same whether the words came in that order or any other. The market fell and fell the market produce identical rows. That is the bag of words assumption, and it is a strong one, yet it survives because for topic level questions the identity of the words carries most of the signal and the order carries less than intuition suggests.
Down-weighting the words that are everywhere

TF-IDF

A term that appears in every document cannot separate documents. Inverse document frequency multiplies each count by how surprising the term is across the corpus, so common words shrink toward zero without anyone writing a stoplist.

Per term: document frequency and idf
The formula in use here

tf(t,d) = number of times term t occurs in document d.

df(t) = number of documents containing t. N = number of documents.

idf(t) = ln(N / df(t)), so a term in every document gets ln(1) = 0 and disappears.

tfidf(t,d) = tf(t,d) × idf(t).

Libraries smooth this. Scikit-learn uses ln((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1 and then normalises each row to unit length, so its numbers will not match these by hand. The shape of the answer is the same: rare terms up, ubiquitous terms down.
There is no single TF-IDF

Weighting schemes, and how much the choice matters

TF-IDF is a family, not a formula. Pick a way to count the term, a way to measure its rarity, and a way to handle document length. Every library makes a different default choice, which is why two implementations disagree on the same corpus.

Comparing two rows

Vector similarity

Hover a cell to see the computation
Cosine similarity between documents
The same idea in two dimensions
horizontal vertical

Each arrow is a document, using only the two chosen terms as coordinates. Cosine measures the angle between arrows, not their length, which is why a long document is not automatically similar to everything.

Why the angle and not the distance

cos(u,v) = (u · v) / (||u|| · ||v||), which is the dot product divided by both lengths.

Double every count in a document, by pasting it to itself, and its Euclidean distance to everything changes while its angle to everything stays identical. Since document length is usually a nuisance rather than the signal, the angle is the honest comparison. On non negative count vectors cosine lies between 0, meaning no shared vocabulary, and 1, meaning identical proportions.

Part 05

From counting to learning: word embeddings

Everything so far represents a word by a column position. Two words are either identical or unrelated, with nothing in between. Embeddings replace that with a short dense vector in which distance means something.

The problem being solved

One-hot is a wall between synonyms

One-hot vector

Length |V|, a single 1. Every pair of distinct words has cosine similarity exactly 0, whether the pair is market and markets or market and elephant.

Dense embedding

Length 2 here, 100 to 1000 in practice, real valued, learned. Nothing is stored per dimension that a human named; the dimensions are whatever the training made useful.

Where the numbers come from

A word is known by the company it keeps

Count which words occur near which, inside a window, and you already have a representation: each row of the co-occurrence table is a vector for that word. This is computed live from your corpus, after the pipeline in Part 01.

From counting to predicting
These rows are long, sparse and mostly zero, exactly the problem from the previous card. Neural embeddings such as word2vec keep the distributional idea and change the method: instead of counting the neighbours, a small network is trained to predict them. Skip-gram takes a word and predicts its context; CBOW takes the context and predicts the word. Neither task is the point. The weights of the hidden layer, one short vector per word, are the point, and the prediction task is only the pressure that forces words used in similar contexts to end up with similar weights.

Read this honestly
The fifteen positions above were chosen by hand so that the arithmetic is visible on a page. A real word2vec or GloVe space is trained on billions of words and has hundreds of dimensions, and the analogy behaviour is weaker and messier than demonstrations suggest. What is real is the property being illustrated: differences between vectors encode relations, so the same offset that separates a capital from its country separates another capital from its country. Neighbours are ranked here by Euclidean distance, since the points were placed by hand; production systems rank by cosine, which gives the same ordering once vectors are normalised to unit length. Also worth stating: these are static embeddings, one vector per word type, so bank gets a single vector for both the river and the money. Contextual models compute a different vector for every occurrence, which is the step beyond this lecture.
