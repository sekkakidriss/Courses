The Taylor rule and r*

How should a central bank set its interest rate? In 1993 John Taylor proposed a one-line answer that described the US Federal Reserve surprisingly well. At its heart sits a number nobody can observe: the neutral interest rate, r*.

Taylor's original rule, with a 2% inflation target and a 2% neutral real rate.

Nominal and real rates

The interest rate that matters for decisions is the one after inflation.

The idea

Suppose a bank pays 5% on a deposit. If prices also rise 5% over the year, your savings buy exactly what they bought before: you gained nothing in real terms. The rate the bank quotes is the nominal interest rate. What it is worth after inflation is the real interest rate.

Households and firms decide whether to borrow, spend or invest based on the real rate. A loan at 8% is expensive when inflation is 1%, and cheap when inflation is 7%. So a central bank that wants to cool the economy has to raise the real rate, not just the nominal one.

The formula

With nominal rate \(i\) and expected inflation \(\pi^e\), the real rate \(r\) is approximately (the Fisher equation, after Irving Fisher):

\[ r = i - \pi^e. \]

Try it

Worked example

A loan costs 6% a year and people expect 4% inflation. The real cost of borrowing is \(6 - 4 = 2\%\). If expected inflation falls to 1% while the loan rate stays at 6%, the real cost jumps to 5%, even though the quoted rate did not change.

Check yourself

The policy rate is 3% and expected inflation is 5%. What is the real rate?
3 - 5 = -2%. A negative real rate means borrowers effectively repay less than they borrowed in purchasing power.

The neutral rate r*

The real interest rate that neither speeds the economy up nor slows it down.

The idea

Think of the real interest rate as the position of a car's accelerator. Press too hard (rates too low) and the economy overheats: output rises above potential and inflation climbs. Lift off too much (rates too high) and it slows: output falls below potential. Somewhere in between is the position that keeps the car at a steady cruising speed. That is the neutral real rate, written r* and also called the natural rate of interest.

Monetary policy is "tight" when the real rate is above r* and "loose" when it is below. A 4% real rate could be tight in one decade and loose in another, depending on where r* is.

The formula

A simple demand relation, like the IS curve from the IS-LM lecture, written for the output gap \(x\) (percent gap between output and potential):

\[ x = -a\,(r - r^*). \]

When \(r = r^*\), the gap is zero. Each point of real rate above r* pushes output \(a\) percent below potential.

Try it

Worked example

The real rate is 3% and \(a = 0.5\). If r* is 1%, policy is tight by 2 points and the gap is \(-0.5 \times 2 = -1\%\). If r* is actually 3%, the same policy is exactly neutral. Getting r* wrong means misjudging the stance.

Check yourself

The real rate is 1% and r* is 2.5%. What is the policy stance?
The real rate is below the neutral rate, so policy stimulates the economy.

The Taylor rule

A recipe: start at neutral, then lean against inflation and the output gap.

The idea

Taylor's rule builds the policy rate in steps. Start from the neutral nominal rate: r* plus current inflation, which keeps the real rate at neutral. Then add a bit more when inflation is above target, to cool things down. And add a bit more when output is above potential, or subtract when it is below.

Taylor did not claim the Federal Reserve used this formula. He showed that from 1987 to 1992 its decisions followed it closely, which made the rule a common benchmark for judging whether policy is too tight or too loose.

The formula

\[ i = r^* + \pi + 0.5\,(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5\,x, \]

with inflation target \(\pi^*\). In Taylor's version \(r^* = 2\%\) and \(\pi^* = 2\%\). Collecting the inflation terms shows the response to inflation is 1.5, more than one for one, which matters in block 4:

\[ i = (r^* - 0.5\,\pi^*) + 1.5\,\pi + 0.5\,x. \]

Try it

Worked example

Inflation is 3% and the output gap is -1%. Then \(i = 2 + 3 + 0.5 \times 1 + 0.5 \times (-1) = 5\%\). The two gaps offset each other, so the rule sets the real rate at its neutral 2%.

Check yourself

With Taylor's numbers, inflation is 6% and the output gap is +2%. What rate does the rule give?
2 + 6 + 0.5 x 4 + 0.5 x 2 = 11%.

The Taylor principle

Why the central bank must move the rate by more than inflation.

The idea

Suppose inflation rises 1 point and the central bank raises its rate by only 0.5. The real rate has fallen by 0.5, so policy has become looser just when inflation is rising. That pushes demand up, inflation rises further, and the process feeds on itself. To stabilise inflation, the nominal rate must rise by more than inflation, so that the real rate rises. This is the Taylor principle.

Many economists read the 1970s this way: central banks raised rates, but by less than inflation, so real rates stayed low or negative and inflation kept climbing.

The formula

A small model economy, quarter by quarter, with \(r^* = 2\%\) and \(\pi^* = 2\%\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= 0.5\,x_{t-1} - 0.5\,(r_{t-1} - r^*) &&\text{demand}\\ \pi_t &= \pi_{t-1} + 0.3\,x_t + u_t &&\text{inflation (adaptive Phillips curve)}\\ i_t &= r^* + \pi^* + \phi_\pi(\pi_t - \pi^*) + 0.5\,x_t, \quad r_t = i_t - \pi_t &&\text{policy} \end{aligned} \]

A cost shock \(u\) pushes inflation up by 1 point in quarter 1. The Taylor principle in this rule means \(\phi_\pi > 1\).

Try it

InflationPolicy rateOutput gap

Worked example

With \(\phi_\pi = 1.5\), a 1-point rise in inflation raises the policy rate by 1.5 points, so the real rate rises 0.5. Demand cools and inflation returns to target. With \(\phi_\pi = 0.8\), the real rate falls 0.2, and inflation drifts away.

Check yourself

Which coefficient on inflation satisfies the Taylor principle?
Only a coefficient above 1 raises the real rate when inflation rises.

Variants in practice

Smoothing, a stronger response to slack, and the floor at zero.

The idea

Real central banks do not jump to wherever a formula points. They move in small steps, usually 0.25 points, to avoid surprising markets and to wait for data, which are noisy and get revised. This is captured by interest rate smoothing: each quarter the rate moves only part of the way towards the rule.

Two other common changes: a larger response to the output gap (often 1.0 instead of 0.5, sometimes called the balanced-approach rule), and a floor near zero, because rates can hardly go much below zero (the effective lower bound).

The formula

\[ i_t = \rho\,i_{t-1} + (1 - \rho)\,i^{\text{rule}}_t, \qquad i_t \ge 0, \]

where \(\rho\) between 0 and 1 measures inertia. Estimates on quarterly data often find values around 0.8.

Try it

A stylised episode over six years: a recession, then an inflation surge, then a slowdown.

InflationOutput gapRule, no floorRate actually set

Notice two things. During the recession the rule asks for a negative rate that cannot be delivered. During the surge, heavy smoothing leaves the rate well behind the rule for many quarters, which is what critics said about several central banks in 2021 and 2022.

Worked example

Last quarter's rate was 2%, the rule now says 6%, and \(\rho = 0.75\). The rate is set at \(0.75 \times 2 + 0.25 \times 6 = 3\%\), closing a quarter of the gap.

Check yourself

The rule prescribes -3% in a deep recession. What does the floor mean for policy?
With the rate stuck at 0 instead of -3%, policy is too tight. That is why central banks used asset purchases and forward guidance after 2008 and in 2020.

Estimating r*

A number nobody observes, inferred from what the economy does.

The idea

We never see r* directly. But we can reason backwards: if the economy is running below potential while the real rate is, say, 1%, then 1% must be above neutral, so r* is lower. Each quarter's output gap gives a rough clue about where r* is. One clue is very noisy, but many clues over time can be averaged.

The question is how to average. If r* barely moves, you should average over many years. If it moves a lot, recent clues deserve more weight. The Kalman filter makes this choice in a principled way, based on how noisy the clues are compared with how fast r* changes. The best-known estimates, by Laubach and Williams, use a Kalman filter on a model like the one in block 2. The next lecture in your path covers the Kalman filter in detail.

The formulas

Rearranging \(x_t = -a\,(r_{t-1} - r^*) + \varepsilon_t\) gives one noisy reading of r* each quarter:

\[ z_t = r_{t-1} + \frac{x_t}{a} = r^* + \frac{\varepsilon_t}{a}. \]

Treat r* as slowly drifting and update the estimate a little towards each new reading:

\[ \hat r^*_t = \hat r^*_{t-1} + K\,(z_t - \hat r^*_{t-1}). \]

The weight \(K\) (the Kalman gain) is large when r* moves a lot relative to the noise, and small when it barely moves. Its value follows from the signal-to-noise ratio: how much r* changes each quarter compared with how noisy each reading is.

Too low a ratio and the estimate lags far behind the true r*. Too high and it chases the noise. The error is smallest in between. Real estimates face the same trade-off, which is why published r* estimates come with very wide uncertainty bands, often several percentage points.

Check yourself

An analyst believes r* almost never changes. What Kalman gain will the filter use?
If r* barely moves, each new noisy reading should change the estimate very little.

Why r* fell

Growth, ageing and saving, and what a low r* means for policy.

The idea

The neutral rate is where the supply of savings meets the demand for investment when the economy is at potential. Anything that raises desired saving or lowers desired investment pushes r* down. Estimates for most advanced economies fell substantially between the 1990s and the late 2010s. The main explanations:

DriverWhy it lowers r*
Slower productivity growthFewer profitable investment projects, and households expect slower income growth, so they borrow less.
Ageing populationsPeople save more for longer retirements, and slower labour force growth needs less new capital.
Global savingHigh saving in some economies flows into world bond markets and pushes rates down everywhere.
Demand for safe assetsInvestors pay a premium for government bonds, lowering their yields relative to riskier returns.

Whether r* has risen again since 2022 is an open debate.

The formula

The link to the Solow lecture comes from households' saving decisions (the Euler equation in the DSGE lecture). On a balanced growth path, roughly:

\[ r^* \approx \rho_d + \frac{1}{\text{EIS}}\,g, \]

where \(\rho_d\) is households' rate of time preference (impatience), \(g\) is growth of output per person, and the EIS (Elasticity of Intertemporal Substitution) measures how willing households are to shift spending over time. Slower growth means a lower r*.

Try it

Set the drivers to see r*, and how much room the central bank has to cut rates before hitting zero.

Worked example

With r* at 0.5% and a 2% target, the neutral nominal rate is 2.5%. In past US recessions the Federal Reserve typically cut rates by around 5 points, so a 2.5% starting point leaves only half the usual room. This is one reason some economists have proposed higher inflation targets.

Check yourself

Productivity growth slows permanently. In this framework, what happens to r*?
Lower g reduces the growth term, and with it the neutral rate.

Fixed exchange rates

When the policy rate is imported: the case of a currency peg.

The idea

Everything so far assumed the central bank chooses its rate. A country that fixes its exchange rate to another currency gives up that choice. If its rates were lower than those of the anchor country, money would flow out in search of higher returns and put pressure on the peg. So in practice, it keeps its rates close to the anchor's.

The UAE dirham has been pegged to the US dollar since the late 1990s, and the Central Bank of the UAE moves its key policy rate in line with the US Federal Reserve. Similar arrangements hold in most Gulf economies. Interest rates are then set for US conditions, which may not match local ones. A Taylor rule computed on local data does not tell you what the rate will be, but it does tell you whether the imported rate is tight or loose for the local economy.

Try it

Set local conditions and the anchor country's policy rate. The local Taylor rule shows what a fully independent central bank would choose.

When the imported rate is too tight or too loose, the remaining tools are fiscal policy (government spending and taxes) and macroprudential measures such as limits on lending, which the IS-LM lecture's fixed-rate case already hinted at.

Check yourself

A pegged economy has a negative output gap and low inflation, while the anchor country raises rates. What happens to the local policy stance?
The peg forces local rates up with the anchor's, just when the local economy needs lower rates.
