The Phillips curve

When jobs are scarce, wages and prices rise slowly. When workers are hard to find, they rise fast. In 1958 the economist A. W. Phillips drew this as a curve, and macroeconomics has argued about it ever since.

The curve is the one Phillips fitted to United Kingdom data from 1861 to 1913. Move the slider.

Measuring inflation

One of the two numbers on the curve. The other, unemployment, was covered in the Okun's law lecture.

The idea

Inflation is the speed at which prices rise in general. Some prices go up, some go down; inflation summarises them all in one number. Statistics offices do this with the CPI (Consumer Price Index): the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services that a typical household buys, tracked over time.

Each item counts according to its weight, its share in household spending. A 10% rise in rent matters more than a 10% rise in the price of matches, because households spend far more on rent.

Try it

A three-item basket. Set how much each price changed over the year and watch the inflation rate.

The formulas

With price index \(P_t\), inflation in percent is

\[ \pi_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \times 100. \]

For a fixed basket, this is approximately the weighted average of the item price changes, with weights \(w_i\) that add up to 1:

\[ \pi_t \approx \sum_i w_i \, \pi_{i,t}. \]

Phillips himself used wage inflation, the growth rate of money wages. Later work switched to price inflation. The two move closely together, because wages are a large part of firms' costs.

Worked example

The CPI was 112.0 last year and 115.36 this year. Inflation is \((115.36 - 112)/112 \times 100 = 3\%\).

Check yourself

Food has weight 0.5 and rises 4%. Everything else has weight 0.5 and rises 2%. What is inflation?
0.5 x 4 + 0.5 x 2 = 3%.

Phillips's discovery

A curved line through half a century of British data.

The idea

Phillips plotted, year by year, the growth of money wages against the unemployment rate. When unemployment was low, wages rose quickly. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly or even fell. The points traced a downward-sloping curve that was steep at low unemployment and flat at high unemployment.

The reason is bargaining power. When few people are looking for work, firms compete for workers by offering higher pay. When many people are looking, workers accept small raises or none.

Try it

The dots below are simulated years scattered around Phillips's fitted curve. Pick an unemployment rate to read the curve.

The formula

Phillips fitted a curve of this form, with \(w\) the wage inflation rate and \(u\) the unemployment rate, both in percent:

\[ w = -0.900 + 9.638\, u^{-1.394}. \]

The negative power makes the curve steep at small \(u\) and nearly flat at large \(u\). Notice where it crosses zero: at about 5.5% unemployment, wages stop rising.

Worked example

At \(u = 2\%\): \(2^{-1.394} \approx 0.381\), so \(w \approx -0.900 + 9.638 \times 0.381 \approx 2.8\%\). Halving unemployment again to 1% gives about 8.7%. The same 1-point fall in unemployment costs far more wage inflation at the low end.

Check yourself

Why is the curve flat when unemployment is high?
Even with many unemployed, money wages rarely fall much, because workers and firms resist nominal cuts. So extra unemployment buys little extra fall in wage growth.

A menu for policy

The 1960s reading: pick the unemployment you want, pay for it in inflation.

The idea

In 1960 Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow redrew the curve for the United States with price inflation instead of wage inflation. It seemed to offer governments a menu. Spend more and run the economy hot: unemployment falls, inflation rises. Cool it down: inflation falls, unemployment rises. Policy was a matter of choosing a point.

The formula

Close to normal conditions, a straight line is a good approximation:

\[ \pi_t = \bar\pi - \kappa\,(u_t - u^*). \]

Here \(\kappa\) (kappa) is the slope: how many points inflation rises when unemployment falls 1 point below the reference level \(u^*\), and \(\bar\pi\) is inflation at that level.

Try it

The next block explains why this menu turned out to be a trap.

Worked example

With \(\bar\pi = 2\%\), \(u^* = 5\%\) and \(\kappa = 0.6\), choosing \(u = 4\%\) gives \(\pi = 2 - 0.6 \times (4 - 5) = 2.6\%\).

Check yourself

With \(\bar\pi = 2\%\), \(u^* = 5\%\) and \(\kappa = 0.5\), what unemployment gives 3.5% inflation on the menu?
3.5 = 2 - 0.5 x (u - 5), so u - 5 = -3 and u = 2%.

Expectations

Friedman and Phelps: people learn, so the menu moves.

The idea

In the late 1960s Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that the menu could not last. Workers care about what their wages can buy. If they expect 5% inflation, they ask for at least 5% raises just to stand still. So the inflation people expect is built into wages and prices.

Suppose the government holds unemployment below normal. Inflation rises a little. Next year people expect that higher inflation, build it into their pay deals, and inflation rises again on top of it. Holding unemployment low does not buy a higher level of inflation. It buys inflation that keeps climbing.

The only unemployment rate at which inflation stays steady is the one where expectations come true. This is the NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment), the same idea as the natural rate \(u^*\).

The formula

\[ \pi_t = \pi^e_t - \kappa\,(u_t - u^*). \]

A simple model of how expectations form is adaptive expectations: people expect this year's inflation to equal last year's, \(\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}\). Substituting gives

\[ \pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -\kappa\,(u_t - u^*). \]

Unemployment now controls the change in inflation, not its level.

Try it

Inflation starts at 2% and \(u^* = 5\%\). The government holds unemployment at the level you choose.

Inflation each yearShort-run curves, years 1, 4, 8, 12Long-run curve at \(u^*\)

Tick the box: inflation stops rising but does not come back down. Expectations have moved up, and getting them down again needs a period of unemployment above \(u^*\). In the long run the curve is vertical at \(u^*\): there is no lasting trade-off.

Worked example

With \(\kappa = 0.5\) and \(u^* = 5\%\), holding \(u = 4\%\) raises inflation by 0.5 points every year: 2.5%, 3%, 3.5%, and so on. After 8 years it is 6%.

Check yourself

Under adaptive expectations, inflation is 4% and unemployment equals \(u^*\). What happens to inflation next year?
At \(u^*\) the change in inflation is zero, so inflation stays wherever expectations are, here 4%.

Supply shocks

The 1970s: inflation and unemployment rose together.

The idea

In 1973 and 1979 oil prices jumped. Firms' costs rose, so they raised prices even though demand was not strong. Inflation went up while unemployment also went up, a combination called stagflation (stagnation plus inflation). The simple menu said this could not happen.

A supply shock shifts the whole curve up. The central bank then faces a hard choice: accept the higher inflation, or push unemployment up to fight it.

The formula

\[ \pi_t = \pi^e_t - \kappa\,(u_t - u^*) + v_t, \]

where \(v_t\) is the supply shock (for example, the extra inflation coming directly from energy prices).

Try it

Worked example

Expected inflation is 2%, \(\kappa = 0.5\), and an oil shock adds \(v = 3\) points. Doing nothing gives 5% inflation at \(u^*\). Keeping inflation at 2% instead requires \(\kappa(u - u^*) = 3\), so unemployment 6 points above \(u^*\). Most central banks choose something in between.

Check yourself

With \(\kappa = 0.5\), how much must unemployment rise above \(u^*\) to fully offset a 2-point supply shock?
We need 0.5 x (u - u*) = 2, so u - u* = 4 points. A flat curve makes fighting supply shocks expensive.

Anchored expectations

Why, from the 1990s until 2020, the curve looked almost flat.

The idea

Since the 1990s, many central banks have announced an inflation target, often 2%, and built credibility by sticking to it. When people believe the target, they expect inflation near 2% whatever happened last year. Expectations are anchored.

Anchoring changes the picture from block 4. A hot economy now pushes inflation up a little, but expectations do not follow, so inflation does not spiral. The trade-off looks flat and stable, which is good news for stability and bad news for anyone trying to read the labour market from inflation.

The formula

A simple way to model partial anchoring mixes the target \(\pi^*\) with last year's inflation:

\[ \pi^e_t = \lambda\,\pi^* + (1 - \lambda)\,\pi_{t-1}, \qquad 0 \le \lambda \le 1. \]

With \(\lambda = 0\) we are back to adaptive expectations. With \(\lambda = 1\) expectations are fully anchored. Modern models write the curve in a forward-looking form, the New Keynesian Phillips curve, where today's inflation depends on expected future inflation and the output gap \(x_t\) (the percentage gap between actual and potential output):

\[ \pi_t = \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\,\pi_{t+1} + \kappa\,x_t. \]

This version is derived in the DSGE lecture.

Try it

Unemployment is held at 4% for 10 years, one point below \(u^*\), with a 2% target.

Your anchoring \(\lambda\)No anchoring (\(\lambda = 0\))

Worked example

With full anchoring (\(\lambda = 1\)), \(\kappa = 0.5\) and unemployment one point below \(u^*\), inflation is \(2 + 0.5 = 2.5\%\) every year. It rises once and stays put, instead of climbing 0.5 points per year.

Check yourself

A central bank loses credibility and \(\lambda\) falls. What happens after an overheating episode?
Lower anchoring means expectations follow past inflation, so each year's rise is built into the next.

Estimating it

Getting \(\kappa\) and the NAIRU from data, and why it is hard.

The idea

With adaptive expectations, the change in inflation is a straight-line function of unemployment. So we can plot, for each year, the change in inflation against unemployment and fit a line. The slope gives \(\kappa\). Where the line crosses zero is the unemployment rate at which inflation is stable: the NAIRU.

The formula

Fit \(\Delta\pi_t = a + b\,u_t + \varepsilon_t\) by OLS (Ordinary Least Squares). Comparing with \(\Delta\pi_t = \kappa u^* - \kappa u_t\):

\[ \kappa = -b, \qquad u^* = -\frac{a}{b}. \]

Why it is hard in practice

ProblemWhat it does
Expectations are not observedAnalysts use surveys, market prices or past inflation as stand-ins, and results depend on the choice.
The NAIRU movesDemographics, skills and labour market rules shift it over time, so one fixed value fits poorly over long periods.
FlatteningAnchored expectations and good policy make the slope small and hard to pin down from data.
Supply shocksEnergy and import prices add noise. Many estimates control for them with extra variables.
Possible nonlinearityThe 2021 to 2023 inflation surge suggested the curve may steepen when labour markets are very tight, as Phillips's original shape implied.

Check yourself

A fitted line is \(\Delta\pi = 2.4 - 0.4\,u\). What are \(\kappa\) and the NAIRU?
The slope is -0.4, so \(\kappa = 0.4\). The NAIRU is 2.4 / 0.4 = 6%.

The cost of disinflation

Bringing inflation down, combined with Okun's law to price it in lost output.

The idea

Once inflation is high and expectations follow it, lowering inflation needs unemployment above \(u^*\) for a while. The Phillips curve tells us how many points of extra unemployment are needed. Okun's law then converts that unemployment into lost output. The ratio of output lost to inflation removed is called the sacrifice ratio.

The formula

Add up the adaptive-expectations equation over the years of the disinflation. Inflation must fall by \(\pi_0 - \pi^*\), so

\[ \sum_t (u_t - u^*) = \frac{\pi_0 - \pi^*}{\kappa} \quad \text{point-years of extra unemployment.} \]

With Okun's coefficient \(\beta\), each point-year costs about \(1/\beta\) percent of a year's GDP (Gross Domestic Product), so

\[ \text{total output loss} \approx \frac{\pi_0 - \pi^*}{\kappa\,\beta}\ \%\ \text{of GDP}, \qquad \text{sacrifice ratio} = \frac{1}{\kappa\,\beta}. \]

Try it

Change "Spread over": the total cost does not change, only how it is distributed. In this simple model, going fast ("cold turkey") and going slowly cost the same. What does lower the cost is credibility: if people believe the new target, expectations fall faster than past inflation, which is the anchoring of block 6.

Worked example

Inflation is 8%, the target is 2%, \(\kappa = 0.5\), \(\beta = 0.5\). Extra unemployment needed: \(6 / 0.5 = 12\) point-years, for example 4 points above \(u^*\) for 3 years. Output loss: \(12 / 0.5 = 24\%\) of one year's GDP in total. Sacrifice ratio: 4% of GDP per point of inflation removed.

Check yourself

With \(\kappa = 0.4\) and \(\beta = 0.5\), what is the sacrifice ratio?
1 / (0.4 x 0.5) = 5: each point of inflation removed costs about 5% of a year's GDP.
