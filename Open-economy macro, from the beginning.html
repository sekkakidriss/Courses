Open-economy macro

Real economies trade goods and move money across borders. That changes how policy works, and it depends heavily on one choice: let the exchange rate float, or fix it.

Exchange rates

The price of one currency in terms of another, and what goods really cost abroad.

The idea

The nominal exchange rate tells you how many units of local currency one unit of foreign currency costs. The UAE dirham, for example, has been fixed at 3.6725 dirhams per US dollar since 1997. In this lecture we quote rates as local currency per foreign currency, so a higher number means the local currency is weaker. A rise is a depreciation; a fall is an appreciation.

For trade, what matters is not the nominal rate alone but how expensive goods are at home compared with abroad. That comparison is the real exchange rate. If local prices rise faster than foreign prices while the nominal rate stays fixed, local goods become relatively more expensive: a real appreciation.

The formula

With nominal rate \(E\) (local per foreign), local price level \(P\) and foreign price level \(P^*\):

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{E\,P^*}{P}. \]

The real exchange rate \(\varepsilon\) is the price of foreign goods in terms of local goods. A higher \(\varepsilon\) means foreign goods are relatively expensive, so local goods are competitive.

Try it

The same basket of goods priced in each country.

Worked example

A basket costs 100 dollars in the US and 400 dirhams in the UAE, with \(E = 3.6725\). In dirhams, the US basket costs 367.25. So \(\varepsilon = 367.25 / 400 \approx 0.92\): the US basket is about 8% cheaper, and local goods are relatively expensive.

Check yourself

The nominal rate is fixed, local inflation is 4% and foreign inflation is 2%. What happens to the real exchange rate over a year?
Local goods get 2% more expensive relative to foreign goods, so \(\varepsilon\) falls by about 2%.

The balance of payments

A country that spends more than it produces must borrow the difference from abroad.

The idea

In an open economy, demand for local output includes what foreigners buy (exports) and excludes what locals buy from abroad (imports). The difference is net exports.

There is a deep accounting link behind this. If a country's saving is larger than its domestic investment, the extra savings go abroad: it lends to the rest of the world and runs a current account surplus. If it invests more than it saves, it must borrow from abroad and runs a deficit. The current account is net exports plus net income from abroad; for simplicity we treat it as net exports here.

The formulas

\[ Y = C + I + G + NX, \qquad NX = X - M. \]

Rearranging, with private saving \(S_p = Y - T - C\) and public saving \(T - G\):

\[ \underbrace{S_p + (T - G)}_{\text{national saving}} - I = NX. \]

Try it

Worked example

Private saving is 20% of GDP, the government runs a deficit of 2% of GDP, and investment is 22%. National saving is \(20 - 2 = 18\%\), so net exports are \(18 - 22 = -4\%\) of GDP: the country borrows 4% of GDP from abroad each year. Oil exporters with large budget surpluses often show the opposite pattern: big current account surpluses.

Check yourself

The government cuts its deficit by 3% of GDP, and private saving and investment do not change. What happens to the current account?
National saving rises by 3 points with investment unchanged, so net exports rise by 3 points.

Prices and trade

A weaker currency helps exports, but often only after a delay.

The idea

A real depreciation makes local goods cheaper for foreigners and foreign goods more expensive for locals. Over time, exports rise and import volumes fall. But the price change is immediate while the quantity changes are slow: contracts are already signed and habits take time to shift. So right after a depreciation, the country pays more for roughly the same imports, and net exports first get worse before they get better. Plotted over time, the path looks like the letter J: the J-curve.

Whether net exports improve in the end depends on how strongly volumes respond. The Marshall-Lerner condition says a depreciation improves the trade balance (starting from balanced trade) if the sensitivities of export and import volumes to the real exchange rate add up to more than 1.

The formula

Measured in local goods, net exports are

\[ NX = X(\varepsilon) - \varepsilon\,M_{\text{vol}}(\varepsilon), \]

where a higher \(\varepsilon\) raises exports and lowers import volume, but also raises the price of each unit imported. The Marshall-Lerner condition is \(\eta_X + \eta_M > 1\), with \(\eta\) the volume sensitivities (elasticities).

Try it

The currency depreciates 10% in real terms in quarter 1. Exports and imports start at 100 each. Volumes adjust gradually over about two years.

Worked example

Right after a 10% depreciation, import volumes are unchanged at 100 but each unit costs 10% more, so the import bill is 110 while exports are still 100: net exports fall to -10. With \(\eta_X = 0.8\) and \(\eta_M = 0.6\), in the long run exports reach 108 and import volume falls to 94, costing \(1.1 \times 94 = 103.4\). Net exports end at about +4.6.

Check yourself

With \(\eta_X = 0.3\) and \(\eta_M = 0.4\), what does a depreciation do to net exports in the long run?
0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7 is below 1, so the Marshall-Lerner condition fails: the higher import price outweighs the volume changes.

Interest parity

With free capital flows, returns must line up across countries.

The idea

An investor can hold a bond at home or abroad. If the foreign bond pays more, money flows abroad, unless the investor expects the foreign currency to weaken by enough to wipe out the extra interest. In equilibrium, with capital moving freely, the expected returns must be equal. This is uncovered interest parity.

The consequence for a fixed exchange rate is direct. If the peg is fully credible, nobody expects the currency to move, so the local interest rate must equal the foreign one. If it were lower, money would leave; if higher, money would pour in. Any gap that remains reflects a risk premium: compensation for the chance that the peg might break.

The formula

\[ i = i^* + \frac{E^e - E}{E} + \text{risk premium}, \]

where \((E^e - E)/E\) is the expected depreciation of the local currency.

Try it

Worked example

The foreign rate is 5%. If markets start to fear a 3% devaluation of a pegged currency, local bonds must pay at least 8% to keep money at home. Defending a peg under suspicion can therefore require very high interest rates, which hurts the local economy.

Check yourself

Under a fully credible peg with free capital flows, the anchor country raises its rate from 4% to 5%. What must the local rate do?
With no expected depreciation, parity requires the local rate to follow the anchor's.

Mundell-Fleming

IS-LM for a small open economy, and why the regime decides which policy works.

The idea

Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming extended IS-LM to an open economy in the early 1960s. Take a small country with free capital flows. By interest parity, its interest rate is tied to the world rate \(i^*\). Net exports join the IS curve and depend on the real exchange rate.

Under a floating rate, a fiscal expansion tends to push the local rate up, which attracts capital, which strengthens the currency, which cuts net exports. The appreciation undoes the fiscal stimulus. A monetary expansion, on the other hand, tends to lower the rate, weakens the currency and boosts net exports: monetary policy is powerful.

Under a fixed rate, it flips. The central bank must keep the exchange rate where it is, so it supplies or withdraws money as needed: monetary policy is no longer its own. But fiscal policy is very effective, because the central bank accommodates it to hold the peg, and nothing is crowded out.

The formulas

Using the IS-LM lecture's numbers, with net exports \(NX = b\,(\varepsilon - 1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= \tfrac{1}{1 - c_1}\left(c_0 - c_1 T + I_0 - d\,i + G + b(\varepsilon - 1)\right) &&\text{IS}\\ \tfrac{M}{P} &= k\,Y - h\,i &&\text{LM}\\ i &= i^* &&\text{interest parity} \end{aligned} \]

Floating: \(M\) is chosen by the central bank, so LM fixes \(Y\), and \(\varepsilon\) adjusts to make IS hold. Fixed: \(\varepsilon\) is fixed, so IS fixes \(Y\), and \(M\) adjusts to make LM hold.

Try it

Worked example

Government spending rises by 60. Floating: output stays at 1500, because the money supply and the world rate pin it down; the currency appreciates until net exports fall by 60. Fixed: output rises to 1650, the full IS-LM multiplier of 2.5, and the central bank expands the money supply from 500 to 575 to hold the peg.

Check yourself

A small open economy with a fixed exchange rate and free capital flows wants to fight a recession. Which tool works?
Under a peg, monetary expansion leaks out as capital outflows, but fiscal expansion raises output with no crowding out.

The trilemma

Three desirable goals, but a country can only have two.

The idea

Block 5 points to a general rule, often called the impossible trinity or trilemma. A country would like a stable exchange rate, free movement of capital, and an independent monetary policy aimed at its own economy. It cannot have all three. Interest parity explains why: with free capital and a fixed rate, the local interest rate must equal the anchor's, so it cannot be set for local conditions.

Try it

Pick any two goals. The third is then out of reach.

Pegs under pressure

Why pegs sometimes break suddenly, before reserves actually run out.

The idea

To defend a peg, a central bank sells foreign currency from its reserves whenever people want to swap local currency for foreign. If the government keeps creating local money, for example to finance a budget deficit, people keep swapping it for foreign currency, and reserves drain.

Paul Krugman's 1979 model showed something surprising: the peg does not die quietly when reserves reach zero. Investors see the end coming. At some point, buying all remaining reserves at the peg rate becomes a sure profit, and a speculative attack wipes them out in one go. The timing is the moment when the shadow exchange rate, the rate that would hold if the currency floated, reaches the peg.

The formulas

A simplified version, in logs. Domestic credit \(D_t\) grows by \(\mu\) per period; reserves are what is left of the money supply, \(R_t = \bar e - D_t\) under the peg \(\bar e\). If the currency floated, it would depreciate at rate \(\mu\), which lowers money demand, so the shadow rate is

\[ \tilde e_t = D_t + \alpha\,\mu, \]

where \(\alpha\) measures how much money demand falls when depreciation is expected. The attack happens when \(\tilde e_t = \bar e\), that is when reserves fall to \(\alpha\mu\), not to zero.

Try it

ReservesShadow exchange ratePeg

Set credit growth to zero: reserves never fall and the peg survives indefinitely. This is why pegs backed by large reserves and disciplined budgets, like those of most Gulf economies, have lasted for decades.

Worked example

Reserves start at 60% of the money supply and credit grows 8 points a year, with \(\alpha = 1.5\). Reserves would reach zero after 7.5 years, but the attack comes when they fall to \(1.5 \times 8 = 12\%\): after \((60 - 12)/8 = 6\) years.

Check yourself

In this model, when does the speculative attack occur?
At that point the attack costs speculators nothing and earns a profit afterwards, so it happens before reserves would run out on their own.

Pegs in practice

What a dollar peg means for an economy like the UAE's.

The idea

A peg to the US dollar brings real benefits: a stable price for trade and investment contracts, and a credible anchor that keeps inflation close to US inflation over time. For oil exporters, oil is priced in dollars, which makes a dollar peg a natural choice.

It also brings constraints, which connect every earlier lecture. Interest rates follow the US Federal Reserve, not local conditions (the Taylor rule lecture). Adjustment to shocks happens through prices instead of the exchange rate: if local inflation runs above US inflation, the real exchange rate appreciates slowly and competitiveness erodes. And fiscal policy becomes the main tool for stabilising the economy, as Mundell-Fleming predicts.

Try it

With the nominal rate fixed, the real exchange rate moves only through inflation differences.

Under a peg, local inflation runs 1.5 points above US inflation for 10 years. What happens to competitiveness?
The real exchange rate falls by roughly 1.5% a year, compounding to about 14% over 10 years.
