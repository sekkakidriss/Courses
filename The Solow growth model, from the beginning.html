The Solow growth model

Why are some countries rich and others poor, and why do rich countries keep getting richer? In 1956 Robert Solow built a model so simple it fits on one line, and it is still where every course on economic growth begins.

Every economy starts with the same small amount of capital per worker. Move the slider and watch where output per worker settles.

Why growth matters

Small differences in growth rates become enormous differences in living standards.

The idea

Growth compounds, like interest in a savings account. An economy that grows 2% a year does not add the same amount each year: it adds 2% of an ever larger total. Over decades, a difference of one or two percentage points separates countries that stay poor from countries that become rich.

Business cycles, the topic of the IS-LM and Okun's law lectures, move output a few percent around its trend. Growth theory is about the trend itself: what determines potential output in the long run.

The formulas

With a constant growth rate \(g\) (as a decimal), output after \(t\) years is

\[ Y_t = Y_0\,(1 + g)^t. \]

The doubling time solves \((1 + g)^t = 2\), so \(t = \ln 2 / \ln(1 + g)\). For small growth rates this is close to the rule of 70: divide 70 by the growth rate in percent.

\[ t_{\text{double}} \approx \frac{70}{g \times 100}. \]

Worked example

At 2% a year, income doubles in about \(70/2 = 35\) years. At 7%, it doubles in 10 years, so it is about \(2^{3.5} \approx 11\) times higher after 35 years. That is roughly the story of the fastest-growing East Asian economies after 1960.

Check yourself

An economy grows 3.5% a year. Roughly how long until income doubles?
70 / 3.5 = 20 years.

Producing output

Capital helps, but each extra machine helps a little less.

The idea

Output is made with capital (machines, buildings, roads) and labour (workers). Give a worker a better tool and she produces more. Give her a second tool and she produces more again, but not as much more: she can only use so many at once. This is diminishing returns to capital, and it drives almost everything in the Solow model.

The formula

The standard choice is the Cobb-Douglas production function, with capital \(K\), labour \(L\) and a number \(\alpha\) between 0 and 1:

\[ Y = K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}. \]

Doubling both capital and labour doubles output (constant returns to scale). That lets us write everything per worker. With \(k = K/L\) (capital per worker) and \(y = Y/L\) (output per worker):

\[ y = k^{\alpha}. \]

The extra output from one more unit of capital, the marginal product of capital, is \(\alpha k^{\alpha - 1}\), which shrinks as \(k\) grows. In data, \(\alpha\) is close to the share of income paid to capital, about one third in most economies.

Try it

The dashed line is the slope at your point: the marginal product. Slide \(k\) to the right and watch it flatten.

Worked example

With \(\alpha = 1/3\), going from \(k = 1\) to \(k = 8\) (eight times more capital per worker) raises output per worker from 1 to \(8^{1/3} = 2\). Eight times the capital only doubles output.

Check yourself

With \(y = k^{1/3}\), capital per worker rises from 8 to 27. What happens to output per worker?
The cube root of 8 is 2 and the cube root of 27 is 3.

Saving and wear

Capital grows when new investment exceeds what wears out.

The idea

Each year, the economy saves a fixed share \(s\) of its income, and those savings are invested in new capital. At the same time, a share \(\delta\) (delta) of existing capital wears out or becomes obsolete: machines break, buildings age. This is depreciation.

Capital per worker rises if investment is larger than depreciation, and falls if it is smaller. Because of diminishing returns, investment grows more slowly than depreciation as capital accumulates. So eventually the two meet.

The formula

For now, assume no population growth and no technological progress. The change in capital per worker is

\[ \Delta k = s\,k^{\alpha} - \delta\,k. \]

The first term is investment per worker; the second is depreciation per worker. This one line is the Solow model.

Try it

The classic Solow diagram. The teal curve is investment, the rose line is depreciation. Pick a level of capital to see which one wins.

Output \(k^\alpha\)Investment \(s k^\alpha\)Depreciation \(\delta k\)

Worked example

With \(s = 0.25\), \(\delta = 0.05\), \(\alpha = 1/3\) and \(k = 8\): investment is \(0.25 \times 2 = 0.5\), depreciation is \(0.05 \times 8 = 0.4\). Capital per worker grows by 0.1 this year.

Check yourself

Investment per worker is 0.6 and depreciation per worker is 0.9. What happens to capital per worker?
New capital does not even replace what wears out, so the stock shrinks by 0.9 - 0.6 = 0.3.

The steady state

Where the economy ends up, and how fast it gets there.

The idea

Where investment equals depreciation, capital per worker stops changing. This is the steady state. From any starting point the economy moves towards it: a poor economy with little capital grows fast, because each new machine adds a lot; a rich economy close to its steady state grows slowly.

This gives one of the model's most famous predictions: convergence. Countries with similar saving rates and technology should grow faster when they are poorer, catching up with richer ones.

The formula

Set \(\Delta k = 0\), so \(s\,k^{\alpha} = \delta\,k\), and solve:

\[ k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}, \qquad y^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}. \]

Near the steady state, the gap closes at a rate of about \(\lambda = (1 - \alpha)\,\delta\) per year, so half the gap is gone after \(\ln 2 / \lambda\) years.

Try it

Start above the steady state (move the starting capital to 20): the economy shrinks towards it, because it cannot afford to maintain so much capital.

Worked example

With \(s = 0.25\), \(\delta = 0.05\) and \(\alpha = 1/3\): \(s/\delta = 5\), so \(k^* = 5^{1.5} \approx 11.2\) and \(y^* = 5^{0.5} \approx 2.24\). The convergence rate is \((2/3) \times 0.05 \approx 3.3\%\) a year, a half-life of about 21 years.

Check yourself

Two countries have the same saving rate, depreciation and technology. Country A has much less capital per worker. What does the model predict?
Same parameters means the same steady state. A is further below it, so its capital earns higher returns and it grows faster.

How much to save

Saving raises the level of income, not its long-run growth, and saving too much is possible.

The idea

A higher saving rate means more capital in the steady state, so more output per worker. But growth only speeds up temporarily, while the economy moves to its new steady state. Once there, growth is zero again in this version of the model. Saving affects the level of income, not its long-run growth rate.

And what people care about is consumption, not output. Saving everything would give huge output and nothing to consume. Saving nothing gives no capital at all. Somewhere in between, steady-state consumption is as high as possible. That saving rate is called the golden rule.

The formula

Steady-state consumption per worker is output minus what must be invested to keep capital constant:

\[ c^* = (1 - s)\,y^* = y^* - \delta\,k^*. \]

Maximising over \(s\) gives a neat answer for Cobb-Douglas production: the golden-rule saving rate equals the capital share, \(s_{\text{gold}} = \alpha\).

Try it

Left: steady-state consumption for every saving rate. Right: what happens over time if the saving rate switches from 25% to your choice in year 10.

Output per workerConsumption per worker

Raise saving from 25% towards the golden rule: consumption drops at first, because people save more, then rises above its old level. Push saving above the golden rule and consumption ends up lower in the long run, even though output is higher.

Worked example

With \(\alpha = 1/3\), \(\delta = 0.05\): at \(s = 0.25\), \(c^* = 0.75 \times 2.24 \approx 1.68\). At the golden rule \(s = 1/3\), \(y^* = (6.67)^{0.5} \approx 2.58\) and \(c^* = (2/3) \times 2.58 \approx 1.72\). At \(s = 0.5\), \(y^* = 10^{0.5} \approx 3.16\) but \(c^* \approx 1.58\).

Check yourself

In the long run of this model (no technological progress), a permanent rise in the saving rate changes...
Growth rises only during the transition. In the new steady state, output per worker is higher but no longer growing.

Population growth

More workers each year means capital is spread more thinly.

The idea

If the number of workers grows at rate \(n\), new workers need machines too. Part of each year's investment just equips them, without raising capital per worker. Population growth works like extra depreciation for capital per worker.

The model therefore predicts that, other things equal, countries with faster population growth have lower income per worker in the steady state. Total output still grows, at rate \(n\), but output per worker does not.

The formula

\[ \Delta k = s\,k^{\alpha} - (n + \delta)\,k, \qquad k^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + \delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}. \]

Try it

Investment \(s k^\alpha\)Break-even \((n + \delta)k\)Without population growth

Worked example

With \(s = 0.25\), \(\delta = 0.05\) and \(n = 0.02\): \(s/(n + \delta) = 3.57\), so \(y^* = 3.57^{0.5} \approx 1.89\), compared with 2.24 when \(n = 0\). Faster population growth lowered steady-state output per worker by about 15%.

Check yourself

Population growth rises. In the new steady state, what grows faster?
Per-worker values are constant in the steady state, so total output grows at the rate of population growth, now faster.

Technology

The only source of lasting growth in income per person.

The idea

So far, output per worker stops growing in the steady state. Yet in rich countries it has grown about 2% a year for over a century. The model's answer: technological progress. Better knowledge, methods and organisation make each worker more productive with the same capital.

With technology improving at rate \(g\), the economy reaches a balanced growth path where capital per worker and output per worker both grow at exactly \(g\). Saving and population growth still decide the level of that path, but only technology decides its slope.

The formula

Let technology \(A\) multiply labour, \(Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}\), with \(A\) growing at rate \(g\). Measure everything per effective worker, \(\hat k = K/(AL)\) and \(\hat y = Y/(AL) = \hat k^{\alpha}\). Then

\[ \Delta \hat k = s\,\hat k^{\alpha} - (n + g + \delta)\,\hat k, \qquad \hat k^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}. \]

Output per worker is \(y = A\,\hat y\). In the steady state \(\hat y\) is constant, so \(y\) grows at the rate of \(A\), which is \(g\).

Try it

The chart uses a logarithmic scale, so constant growth appears as a straight line. The economy starts below its balanced growth path.

Worked example

With \(g = 2\%\), output per worker doubles roughly every 35 years along the balanced growth path, whatever the saving rate. Raising the saving rate shifts the straight line up; it does not tilt it.

Check yourself

On the balanced growth path, what is the growth rate of total output?
Output per worker grows at g and the number of workers grows at n, so total output grows at about n + g.

Growth accounting

Splitting actual growth into capital, labour and the part nobody can measure directly.

The idea

Solow also used his production function on data. Output grows because capital grows, because labour grows, and because of everything else that makes inputs more productive. The first two can be measured. The third is what is left over: the Solow residual, also called TFP (Total Factor Productivity) growth. Solow found that in the United States most growth in output per hour came from this residual, not from more capital.

The formula

Taking growth rates of \(Y = A K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}\) (here \(A\) multiplies everything, the usual convention for growth accounting):

\[ g_Y = \alpha\,g_K + (1 - \alpha)\,g_L + g_A \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_A = g_Y - \alpha\,g_K - (1 - \alpha)\,g_L. \]

Try it

Worked example

Output grows 3%, capital 4%, labour 1%, with \(\alpha = 1/3\). Capital contributes \(4/3 \approx 1.33\) points and labour \(2/3 \approx 0.67\) points. The residual is \(3 - 1.33 - 0.67 = 1.0\) point: a third of growth is unexplained by inputs.

What the model gets right and wrong

PredictionEvidence
Convergence among similar economiesHolds well among regions of a country and among rich countries (conditional convergence). Fails across all countries, because steady states differ.
Higher saving, higher income levelSupported: high-investment economies tend to be richer.
Faster population growth, lower income per workerBroadly supported across countries.
Long-run growth comes from technologySupported, but the model takes technology as given. Endogenous growth models (Romer, 1990) explain it through research and ideas.
Capital explains income differencesOnly partly. Adding human capital (education) helps a lot, as Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992) showed, but large TFP differences remain.
Link to the other lectures: the balanced growth path is what statistics offices and central banks call potential output, the \(Y^*\) in Okun's law and the natural output \(Y_n\) in AD-AS. Business cycles are fluctuations around this path.

Check yourself

Output grows 4%, capital 6%, labour 0%, with \(\alpha = 0.5\). What is TFP growth?
4 - 0.5 x 6 - 0.5 x 0 = 1%.
