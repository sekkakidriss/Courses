Regression

Every regression in the world answers the same question: given what I know, what is the typical value of what I care about? The dozens of names, logistic, Poisson, ridge, quantile, exist because three things change: the nature of the outcome, the shape of the relationship, and the trouble in the data. Note 00

One line, one promise, one measure of failure

Before any family of models, one machine. We will build it on a single example and never leave it: everything on the next three notes is this same machine with one of its three parts swapped out.

Beat 1 · Intuition

The promise a regression makes, and the one it does not

You have 60 apartments. For each one you know its size in square metres and the price it sold for. Someone walks in and says: my apartment is 95 square metres, what is it worth?

You cannot answer exactly. Two apartments of 95 square metres sell for different prices, because of the floor, the view, the year, the negotiation. But you can answer on average. That is the whole promise:

The promise
A regression does not predict the value of one case. It estimates the average value of the cases that look like it. In symbols, it models the conditional mean.
Read it aloud: "the expected value of Y, given that X equals x, is some function f of x." Y is what you want to know (price), X is what you already know (size), and f is the recipe you are about to choose.

Choosing a regression model is exactly two decisions: which family of shapes do I allow for f, and how do I measure the mistake so that I can pick the best member of that family.

Beat 3 · The formula behind the button
The mistake on apartment i is the residual eᵢ = yᵢ − (β₀ + β₁xᵢ). We square each one, so that a miss of +10 and a miss of −10 both count as failures, and add them up. That total is what the button minimises.
Setting the two derivatives to zero gives a closed form, no searching needed. This is OLS, Ordinary Least Squares.
The slope is the co-movement of x and y divided by the movement of x alone, and the line is forced through the centre of gravity of the cloud, the point (x̄, ȳ). With several predictors the same algebra in matrix form gives β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy.
Why squares, and not absolute values
Squaring is a choice, not a law. It has one beautiful property, it makes the answer a conditional mean, and one ugly one, a single far away point can drag the whole line, because its error is squared. Minimising absolute values instead gives the conditional median and ignores extremes. Note 02 turns that choice into a model of its own, and note 03 uses it to survive outliers.

Note 01 · Part A of the machine

The nature of Y decides the model

This is the first question to ask, and it eliminates nine tenths of the catalogue immediately. A straight line is free to output any number it likes: 3.7, minus 400, two million. If your outcome is a probability, a category or a count of accidents, that freedom is a bug. The fix is one small idea, repeated seven times.

Beat 1 · Intuition

Watch a straight line fail on a yes / no outcome

Sixty loan applicants. The horizontal axis is monthly income, the vertical axis is the outcome: 1 if the loan defaulted, 0 if it did not. There are only two heights in the whole picture. What we really want is not the 0 or the 1, it is the probability of a 1 at that income, and probabilities live between 0 and 1.

Beat 2 · The trick, in one line
Keep the straight part, then squeeze the result into the interval it belongs to. Call the straight part the linear predictor.
For a probability, squeeze η through the logistic function, which maps the whole real line into (0, 1) and never leaves it.
The right hand version is the useful one for teaching: the model is a straight line, it is simply a straight line in the log-odds rather than in the probability. That transformation is called the link function, here the logit link.
How to read the coefficient
One extra unit of x multiplies the odds by e^β₁. If β₁ = −0.4, then e^−0.4 ≈ 0.67: each extra thousand of income cuts the odds of default by about a third. Note the word odds, not probability. The effect on the probability itself depends on where you stand on the curve, which is why the curve is steep in the middle and flat at the ends.
Beat 3 · What the fit button minimises
Squared error is meaningless for a 0/1 outcome, so we change the measure of failure. We ask: which β makes the data we actually observed most probable? That is maximum likelihood, and its negative logarithm is the log loss, the same quantity machine learning calls cross entropy.
Each observation contributes the log of the probability the model gave to what actually happened. A confident wrong answer is punished brutally, because log of a small number is a large negative. There is no closed form here, so the computer iterates, but the idea has not changed: choose the parameters that make the total failure smallest.
Beat 4 · The same trick, three destinations

The link function, seen as a funnel

Drag the linear predictor η. The three funnels show what each link does with the same number. This one picture is the entire GLM family, Generalized Linear Model: a straight line inside, a squeeze on the way out.

The general form
A GLM has three ingredients, and naming them is how you name the model.
1. a distribution for Y (normal, Bernoulli, Poisson, ...), 2. a linear predictor Xβ, 3. a link g connecting the two. Ordinary linear regression is the case where the distribution is normal and the link is the identity, g(μ) = μ, which is why it looks like it has no link at all.
Beat 5 · Counts

When Y is a number of events, the spread is not constant

Number of workplace accidents per firm per month, against firm size. Counts cannot be negative and they cannot be 2.7. Something else happens too, which the slider will show you: big counts are naturally more variable than small counts. A model with one constant error size cannot be right.

Read the fit
Poisson regression
Force the mean to be positive by putting the straight line in the exponent. The link is the logarithm.
Because the line sits in the exponent, coefficients are read as multipliers: one more unit of x multiplies the expected count by e^β₁. A coefficient of 0.10 means about 10 percent more accidents per unit.
The assumption that usually breaks, and its repair
The Poisson distribution has one parameter doing two jobs: Var(Y) = E[Y] = λ. Real count data are almost always more spread out than that, because firms differ in ways you did not measure. Push the dispersion slider and watch the points escape the band. The repair keeps the same mean model and gives the variance its own knob.
As θ → ∞ the negative binomial becomes the Poisson again, so nothing is lost by allowing it. If instead the problem is a pile of exact zeros, for example cigarettes smoked in a population where most people do not smoke at all, use a zero inflated model: one sub-model for "is this person a smoker at all", one for "how many".

Note 02 · Part B of the machine

The shape of the link between X and Y

Note 01 fixed the exit of the machine. Now we open the middle. "Linear" never meant that the picture is a straight line, it meant that the model is linear in the coefficients. That distinction is what lets one family of algebra fit curves, steps, bumps and thresholds.

Beat 1 · Intuition

A straight line is a guess about the world, not a neutral default

Below, a real looking relationship that bends: the yield of a process against temperature. It rises, peaks, then falls. A straight line through it is not slightly wrong, it is wrong in a specific and dangerous way: it will tell you that more temperature is always better.

Slide the degree. Degree 1 is a straight line. Degree 2 adds a squared term and can bend once. Degree 12 can pass through almost every point, which sounds like victory and is a disaster. The two bars keep score on data the model has seen (train) and on data it has never seen (test, the hollow points).

Beat 2 · Polynomial regression
Nothing new is needed. Build extra columns from the column you have, then run the same least squares as note 00.
This is still a linear model, because it is linear in β. The curve is in the picture, not in the algebra. The same idea covers log(x), 1/x, interactions x₁x₂, and dummies for categories.
The trade-off you just watched
Prediction error splits into three pieces, and the degree slider moves the first two in opposite directions.
Bias is being systematically wrong because the shape is too rigid, the straight line on a curve. Variance is answering differently every time the sample changes, the degree 12 wiggle. The last term is the noise nobody can remove. Press "New sample" a few times at degree 12 and watch the curve change wildly while the data barely changes: that is variance, made visible.
Beat 3 · A better way to bend

Splines: local pieces instead of one global formula

A high degree polynomial is bent everywhere by everything: one point on the left changes the curve on the right. A spline fixes this by cutting the axis at a few knots and letting each piece have its own slope, while forcing the pieces to join without a break. Move the knots slider and watch the basis functions appear underneath.

The small panel shows the columns actually handed to least squares: one hockey stick per knot.
Read the fit
The truncated power basis
With knots κ₁ < ... < κᴸ, add one column per knot that is zero until the knot and then rises.
Again ordinary least squares, only the columns changed. Each γₖ is the change in slope at knot k. Replacing the hockey sticks with cubic pieces gives the usual smooth cubic spline; adding one such smooth term per variable and summing them gives a GAM, Generalized Additive Model, written g(E[Y]) = β₀ + f₁(x₁) + ... + fₚ(xₚ). You keep the readability of one curve per variable and lose the straight line straitjacket.
Beat 4 · Change the question, not the shape

Quantile regression: the average is not always what you want

Rents against apartment size again, but now with a twist that is everywhere in real data: the cheap end and the expensive end do not behave the same way. The mean line answers "what is typical". It cannot answer "what does the bottom 10 percent of the market do", which is often the actual policy question.

Move τ from 0.1 to 0.9. The line does not change shape, it changes which part of the distribution it tracks. At the end, tick "show all" to see the fan.

Read the fit
quantile τ OLS mean
The pinball loss
Only the penalty changes. Under-predicting costs τ per unit, over-predicting costs 1−τ per unit. The asymmetry is what pulls the line up or down.
At τ = 0.5 the two costs are equal, the loss becomes the absolute value, and the fit is the conditional median, the robust cousin of the mean from note 00. The lines are not required to be parallel, and when they are not, that itself is the finding: the effect of size on rent is different for cheap and expensive apartments.
Beat 5 · Give up on formulas altogether

Regression trees: cut the space into boxes and use the average in each box

Sometimes the relationship has no smooth shape at all: below a threshold nothing happens, above it everything does, and the effect of one variable depends on another. A tree makes no assumption about shape. It repeatedly asks a yes/no question that splits the data into two groups as different as possible, then predicts the plain average inside each final box.

Dashed verticals are the cuts the algorithm chose. The prediction is the flat step inside each region.
The splits it chose
What a split optimises
At each step the algorithm scans every variable and every possible cut point s, and keeps the one that reduces the squared error most.
Then it repeats inside each half. A single tree is easy to read and unstable: change a few points and the cuts move. Averaging hundreds of trees grown on resampled data fixes the instability, that is a random forest; growing them in sequence so each one repairs the previous one's residuals is gradient boosting. You gain accuracy and you lose the coefficient with a meaning, which is exactly the trade you must decide on before choosing.

Note 03 · Part C of the machine

Trouble in the data, and the model that survives it

The last family of names has nothing to do with Y and nothing to do with the shape. These models exist because real data misbehave: too many columns, one absurd point, observations that come in clusters, observations that come in order, and inputs that are themselves caused by the outcome. Each problem breaks a specific assumption, and each has a specific repair.

Beat 1 · Too many predictors

Ridge and LASSO: pay a price for every coefficient you use

Eight candidate predictors, only three of which truly matter, and two of the others are near copies of each other. Ordinary least squares at λ = 0 hands you eight large coefficients with confident opposite signs, most of them noise. Slide the penalty λ up and watch the bars collapse toward zero.

The difference between the two is worth seeing directly: ridge shrinks everything and keeps everything, LASSO sets coefficients exactly to zero and therefore chooses variables for you.

Above: the coefficient path, each line is one predictor as the penalty grows. Below: the coefficients at the current λ. Teal bars are the three genuinely useful predictors.
The two penalties
Same fit term, different price list. The penalty is added to the sum of squared errors, so the model must justify every unit of coefficient it spends.
Ridge has a closed form, and the λI term is literally what makes the matrix invertible again when predictors are collinear or when p > n.
The squared penalty has a round contour, so the solution slides toward zero without touching it. The absolute value penalty has corners on the axes, and corners are where solutions land, which is why LASSO produces exact zeros. Elastic Net uses λ[αΣ|β₃| + (1−α)Σβ₃²] and is the sensible default when predictors come in correlated groups. Always standardise the columns first, otherwise the penalty punishes whichever variable happens to be measured in small units.
The bargain you are accepting
Penalised coefficients are biased on purpose. You accept a small systematic error in exchange for a large reduction in variance, and the total prediction error goes down. This also means penalised coefficients are not the right tool for a careful causal statement about one variable, they are a tool for prediction and for variable screening. Choose λ by cross validation, never by eye.
Beat 2 · One bad point

Robust regression: drag the red point and watch OLS lose its mind

Grab the red apartment and drag it anywhere, especially far up and to the right. The dashed violet line is ordinary least squares. The solid line is a robust fit. Squaring the error is what makes OLS so sensitive: a point ten times further away counts a hundred times more.

Slopes right now
OLS Huber, robust line without the red point
Huber loss
Behave like least squares near zero, where the errors are ordinary, and like absolute value far away, where the errors are suspicious.
Beyond the threshold δ the penalty grows only linearly, so an extreme point can no longer buy the line. In practice this is fitted by re-weighted least squares: fit, look at who is far away, give them a smaller weight, refit, repeat. Do not delete outliers silently. Fit both, report both, and if they disagree go and look at that observation.
Beat 3 · Observations that come in clusters

Mixed models: neither one line for everyone nor a line per group

Pupils inside schools, firms inside emirates, repeated measurements on the same patient. Observations inside a cluster resemble each other, which breaks the assumption that every row brings fresh information. Two naive answers exist and both are bad. The third is the model.

What each answer does
Random intercepts
Give each group its own intercept, but declare that these intercepts are themselves draws from a distribution instead of free parameters.
The consequence is shrinkage: each group's estimate is pulled toward the overall mean by an amount that depends on how little data that group has.
A group with three observations is barely trusted and gets pulled almost all the way back to the common line. A group with two hundred keeps its own position. Drag the sample size slider on group 4 and watch its line travel between the two extremes. The share of variance sitting between groups, ICC = τ²/(τ²+σ²), tells you whether you needed this model at all.
Beat 4 · Observations that come in order

Time: yesterday's error tells you about today's

The same regression run on a time series usually leaves residuals that drift in waves rather than scattering. The coefficients may still be usable, but the standard errors are wrong, often by a factor of two or three, and every test built on them is optimistic. Slide the autocorrelation and watch the residual plot change from confetti to a wave.

Diagnostics
AR(1) errors
The Durbin-Watson statistic DW ≈ 2(1−ρ) is the quick check: near 2 is clean, near 0 means strong positive autocorrelation. Repairs, in increasing order of ambition: keep OLS but use HAC standard errors (Newey-West); add lagged terms and model the dynamics (ARIMA with regressors, distributed lags); or, if both series trend, test for unit roots first with the ADF test, Augmented Dickey-Fuller, and if they are cointegrated use an error correction model. Regressing one trending series on another without that check is the classic spurious regression, which produces a beautiful R² and means nothing.
Beat 5 · The deepest trouble

When X is not innocent: endogeneity and instrumental variables

Every model above estimates an association. It becomes a causal effect only if the predictor is unrelated to everything you left out. Here, an unmeasured factor (call it ability) pushes both the years of education and the wage. Slide its strength and watch the OLS slope drift away from the true effect, which is fixed at 1.0 and drawn in grey.

Estimated effect of education on wage
Two stage least squares
An instrument Z is a variable that moves X but has no other route to Y. Use only the part of X that Z explains, because that part cannot contain the hidden factor.
Two conditions, one testable and one not. Relevance: Z really moves X, which you check with the first stage F statistic, above 10 by the usual rule of thumb. Push the instrument strength slider down and watch the estimate become unstable, that is the weak instrument problem. Exclusion: Z affects Y only through X, which no test can prove and which you must defend with an argument about the world. Difference in differences, regression discontinuity and fixed effects are the other members of this family, all answering the same worry.
Closing

The order of operations

StepWhat you do
1. Look at YIts nature removes most of the catalogue at once. Number, yes/no, category, count, proportion, duration.
2. State the goalExplanation wants interpretable coefficients and standard errors. Prediction wants out of sample error. They lead to different models.
3. Fit the simplest thingThe straight line inside the right family. It is your reference point, and often the final answer.
4. Look at the residualsThey should be structureless. A curve means the shape is wrong (note 02). A fan means the spread is wrong (note 01). A wave means time (note 03).
5. Add complexity only against evidenceEvery repair on note 03 costs something. Pay only when the diagnostic asked you to.
6. Validate honestlyOut of sample or cross validated error. The error on the points the model has already seen is not evidence, as the degree 12 curve showed.
