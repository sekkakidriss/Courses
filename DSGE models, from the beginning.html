DSGE models

A Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium model is a small artificial economy written in equations. Central banks use them to ask: if an unexpected event hits today, what happens to output, inflation and interest rates over the next few years?

A three-equation New Keynesian model, the core of most DSGE models, solved live. By block 6 you will know every piece of it.

The four words

The name is long, but each word adds one specific ingredient.

The idea

Older forecasting models were large sets of equations fitted to past data, for example "consumption equals 0.8 times income". In 1976 Robert Lucas pointed out a problem, now called the Lucas critique: those coefficients summarise how people behaved under past policies. If policy changes, people change their behaviour, and the coefficients break.

DSGE models answer this by starting from decisions. Households and firms are described by what they want and what limits them. Their behaviour is derived from those goals, so when policy changes, the model recomputes how they respond. Click each word.

Households

People like a smooth standard of living, and interest rates decide when they spend.

The idea

Imagine you will earn a lot this year and little next year. Most people would not spend everything now and starve later. They save some of this year's income to spread spending across both years. This is consumption smoothing.

The interest rate changes the deal. A higher rate makes saving more rewarding: every unit saved today buys more tomorrow. So people tend to spend a bit less now and more later.

Try it

A household lives two years. Set its income in each year and the interest rate. Its patience is fixed: it values next year's enjoyment at 96% of this year's.

IncomeConsumption chosen

Try swapping the two incomes: consumption barely moves, because only total lifetime resources matter. Then raise the interest rate: consumption shifts towards next year.

The formula

The household chooses consumption \(C_t\) to maximise lifetime enjoyment, with patience \(\beta\) (here 0.96) and real interest rate \(r\). The optimal choice satisfies the Euler equation (here with log utility, a common simple choice):

\[ \frac{1}{C_t} = \beta\,(1 + r_t)\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{1}{C_{t+1}}\right]. \]

In words: the enjoyment lost by giving up one unit today must equal the discounted enjoyment gained from \((1 + r)\) units tomorrow. \(\mathbb{E}_t\) means "expected, given what is known today".

In a DSGE model, output equals consumption in this simple version, so this equation becomes a demand equation for the whole economy. Written for the output gap \(x_t\) (percent gap between output and potential), it is called the dynamic IS curve (IS stands for Investment and Saving, an older name for the demand side):

\[ x_t = \mathbb{E}_t\,x_{t+1} - \frac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t - \mathbb{E}_t\,\pi_{t+1} - r^n_t\right). \]

Here \(i_t\) is the nominal interest rate set by the central bank, \(i_t - \mathbb{E}_t\pi_{t+1}\) is the real interest rate, \(r^n_t\) is the natural rate (the real rate that would keep output at potential), and \(\sigma\) measures how strongly spending reacts. A real rate above its natural level makes people postpone spending, and output falls below potential.

Worked example

Income is 120 this year and 40 next year, the interest rate is 5%. Lifetime resources are \(120 + 40/1.05 \approx 158.1\). With \(\beta = 0.96\) and log utility, the household spends \(158.1 / 1.96 \approx 80.7\) this year, saves about 39.3, and spends \(0.96 \times 1.05 \times 80.7 \approx 81.3\) next year.

Check yourself

In the dynamic IS curve, the central bank raises the real interest rate above the natural rate. What happens to the output gap today?
Saving becomes more attractive, households spend less today, and output falls below potential.

Firms and sticky prices

Why money and interest rates affect real output at all.

The idea

If every firm could change its prices instantly, a change in interest rates would just change prices, and output would not move. In reality, many prices are fixed for months: menus, catalogues, contracts. Economists call this price stickiness.

The standard way to model it, due to Guillermo Calvo (1983), is a lottery. Each quarter, only a random share of firms gets to reset its price. The rest keep last quarter's price. Because firms know their price may be stuck for a while, those who can reset look ahead: they set a price that will be right on average over the coming quarters. That is why expected future inflation appears in the equation below.

Try it

All firms' costs rise permanently by 10%. Choose the share of firms that are stuck each quarter and watch the average price catch up.

The formula

Aggregating the firms' decisions gives the New Keynesian Phillips curve:

\[ \pi_t = \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\,\pi_{t+1} + \kappa\,x_t + u_t. \]

Inflation today depends on expected inflation tomorrow and on the output gap, because a booming economy pushes up wages and other costs. \(u_t\) is a cost-push shock, such as an energy price jump. The slope \(\kappa\) depends on stickiness. In the simplest setup,

\[ \kappa = \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta\theta)}{\theta}\times(\text{a term for how costs respond to output}). \]

Stickier prices (higher \(\theta\)) give a smaller \(\kappa\): inflation reacts less to the output gap, and monetary policy has bigger effects on output.

Worked example

With \(\theta = 0.75\), a quarter of firms reset each quarter, so prices last \(1/(1 - 0.75) = 4\) quarters on average, about a year. This is close to what studies of individual price records find for many economies.

Check yourself

If \(\theta = 0.5\), how long does a price last on average?
Half of firms reset each quarter, so the average duration is 1 / (1 - 0.5) = 2 quarters.

The central bank

A simple rule for setting the interest rate closes the model.

The idea

Households and firms react to the interest rate, but who sets it? In 1993 John Taylor showed that the United States Federal Reserve's decisions were described well by a simple rule: raise the rate when inflation is above target or the economy runs hot, cut it when the opposite happens. DSGE models usually include such a Taylor rule.

One detail is crucial. When inflation rises by 1 point, the central bank must raise the nominal rate by more than 1 point, so that the real rate actually rises and cools the economy. This is the Taylor principle.

The formula

Taylor's original rule, in percent per year, with a 2% inflation target and a 2% neutral real rate:

\[ i_t = 2 + \pi_t + 0.5\,(\pi_t - 2) + 0.5\,x_t. \]

In DSGE models it is written in deviations from steady state, with a policy shock \(v_t\):

\[ i_t = \phi_\pi\,\pi_t + \phi_x\,x_t + v_t, \qquad \phi_\pi > 1. \]

Try it

Worked example

Inflation is 4% and the output gap is -2%. Taylor's rule gives \(i = 2 + 4 + 0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times (-2) = 6\%\). The real rate is \(6 - 4 = 2\%\): the high inflation and the weak economy pull in opposite directions and cancel.

Check yourself

A central bank uses \(\phi_\pi = 0.8\). Inflation rises by 1 point. What happens to the real interest rate?
The nominal rate rises 0.8 while inflation rises 1, so the real rate falls 0.2. Policy loosens just when it should tighten, which violates the Taylor principle.

Shocks

The "stochastic" part: what keeps the model economy moving.

The idea

Without surprises, the model economy would sit at its steady state forever. Shocks push it away: a sudden burst of consumer confidence, an energy price jump, an unexpected interest rate decision. Most shocks fade gradually rather than vanishing at once, so they are modelled as persistent processes.

The formula

The workhorse is the AR(1) (first-order autoregressive) process:

\[ z_t = \rho\,z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2). \]

Each period, the shock keeps a share \(\rho\) (rho) of last period's value and receives a fresh random kick \(\varepsilon_t\) with standard deviation \(s\). With \(\rho\) close to 1, shocks last a long time. After a single kick, the shock decays as \(\rho^t\).

Try it

Simulated shockDecay after one kick of size 1

Worked example

With \(\rho = 0.8\), a shock of size 1 is worth 0.8 after one quarter, 0.64 after two, and about 0.33 after five. Its half-life is \(\ln 0.5 / \ln 0.8 \approx 3.1\) quarters.

Check yourself

With \(\rho = 0.5\), how much of a shock is left after 3 quarters?
0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.125, so 12.5%.

Equilibrium

Putting the three equations together and solving them.

The idea

We now have three equations, one per actor. Each contains variables the others determine. Households' spending depends on the interest rate. The interest rate depends on inflation. Inflation depends on spending and on what everyone expects. Equilibrium means finding paths for all three that satisfy every equation at once, with expectations that are consistent with the model itself (rational expectations).

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \mathbb{E}_t x_{t+1} - \tfrac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t - \mathbb{E}_t\pi_{t+1}\right) + d_t &&\text{households (demand)}\\ \pi_t &= \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\pi_{t+1} + \kappa\,x_t + u_t &&\text{firms (supply)}\\ i_t &= \phi_\pi\,\pi_t + \phi_x\,x_t + v_t &&\text{central bank} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(d_t\), \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) are demand, cost-push and monetary shocks, each following an AR(1) with persistence \(\rho\).

How it is solved

For one shock \(z_t\) at a time, guess that the solution is proportional to it: \(x_t = a\,z_t\) and \(\pi_t = b\,z_t\). Since \(\mathbb{E}_t z_{t+1} = \rho z_t\), the expectations become \(\rho a z_t\) and \(\rho b z_t\). Substituting into the three equations leaves two ordinary linear equations in \(a\) and \(b\), solved exactly. This is the method of undetermined coefficients. Software such as Dynare (a free tool for solving and estimating DSGE models) does the same for models with dozens of equations.

The solution is unique only if policy reacts strongly enough to inflation. For this model the condition is

\[ \kappa\,(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\,\phi_x > 0, \]

essentially the Taylor principle from block 4.

Things to try: with the cost-push shock, raise \(\phi_\pi\) and watch the central bank trade a deeper fall in output for less inflation. Then push \(\phi_\pi\) below 1 and see the model lose its unique solution.

Worked example

Monetary tightening with \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\sigma = 1\), \(\kappa = 0.1\), \(\phi_\pi = 1.5\), \(\phi_x = 0.125\), \(\rho = 0.5\) and a shock of 0.25. The coefficients solve \(a(1 - \rho + \phi_x/\sigma) + b(\phi_\pi - \rho)/\sigma = -0.25/\sigma\) and \(-\kappa a + b(1 - \beta\rho) = 0\). This gives \(a \approx -0.30\) and \(b \approx -0.06\): output falls 0.30% below potential and quarterly inflation falls 0.06 points on impact, then both fade at rate 0.5 per quarter.

Check yourself

After a cost-push shock, why does output fall even though nothing happened to demand?
Higher inflation triggers a more than one-for-one rise in the nominal rate, so the real rate rises and spending falls. This is the trade-off a cost shock creates.

Giving it numbers

Calibration and Bayesian estimation.

The idea

The model needs values for its parameters. There are two approaches. Calibration picks values from other evidence: average interest rates for \(\beta\), studies of price records for \(\theta\), long-run ratios in national accounts. Estimation fits the parameters to time series such as GDP, inflation and interest rates.

Most modern DSGE estimation is Bayesian. You start with a prior: what you believe about a parameter before seeing the data, from theory or earlier studies. The data then provide a likelihood: which values make the observed history most probable. Combining the two gives the posterior, an updated belief. When the data are informative, they dominate. When they say little, the prior dominates.

In a full model there are many parameters at once, and the posterior is explored by simulation (a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler), with a Kalman filter computing the likelihood of the data. The logic is the same as in this one-parameter picture.

Check yourself

The data are very noisy about \(\theta\) (large data uncertainty). Where does the posterior end up?
Noisy data carry little precision, so the precision-weighted average leans on the prior. This is why priors matter so much in DSGE estimation.

Uses and limits

What DSGE models are good for, and the main criticisms.

What they are used for

UseExample question
Policy analysisWhat happens to inflation and output if the central bank raises rates by 0.25 points?
Telling stories about the dataWas the last slowdown mainly a demand shock or a supply shock? (shock decomposition)
Comparing rulesWould a rule that reacts more strongly to inflation make the economy more stable?
ForecastingUsed alongside statistical models, with the advantage that every forecast comes with an economic explanation.

Main criticisms

CriticismWhat it meansResponse in newer models
Missing financeEarly models had no banks or credit, so they could not explain the 2008 crisis.Financial frictions, bank balance sheets, borrowing limits.
One representative householdEveryone is identical, so inequality and hand-to-mouth spending are ignored.HANK models (Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian) with many different households.
Rational expectationsPeople are assumed to know the model and forecast perfectly on average.Learning, limited attention, survey-based expectations.
Linear approximationsSmall shocks around a steady state; large crises and the zero lower bound on rates are hard to capture.Nonlinear solution methods, occasionally binding constraints.
Weak identificationThe data often cannot separate parameters, so priors drive results.Careful prior choice, more data series, micro evidence.

Check yourself

Why are DSGE models said to answer the Lucas critique?
Because decisions are derived from preferences and technology, a new policy rule leads the model's agents to re-optimise, rather than relying on coefficients from a past regime.
