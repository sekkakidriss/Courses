Graph Theory:
Algorithms

Part 1 gave you the vocabulary: vertices, edges, weights, directions, trees. This part is about questions. Every graph algorithm answers one precise question about a network, and the whole subject fits in six of them: how do I get around, what is the cheapest route, how do I connect everything, how much can I push through, in what order, and where does it break. Step through each panel rather than reading it.

Part 02

Traversal and search: BFS and DFS

You met both in Part 1. Here they earn their living. BFS expands in rings and therefore finds the shortest route in number of steps. DFS plunges down one branch and therefore builds mazes, detects cycles, and explores every possibility one at a time. Everything else in this lecture is a traversal with a twist.

Why BFS is the right ghost
BFS labels every tile with its distance from the source before it labels any tile further away. The first time it touches the target, the label on the target is the true shortest distance, and following labels downward, d, d-1, d-2, …, 0, is the path. DFS also finds a path, and often finds it after exploring fewer tiles, but the path it returns is whatever branch it happened to be on.
DFS builds the maze in the first place

Layers are the whole point

            When a social network shows you "3 mutual friends" or "2nd degree connection", it has run BFS from you and read the layer number. Layer 1 is your friends, layer 2 your friends' friends. Because BFS finishes a whole layer before starting the next, the layer number is the shortest distance, no separate search required.
          

        

      

    


    

      
Where you have seen it

      

        
Arcade AI
Pac-Man ghosts
Ghosts pick their next tile by comparing distances to a target tile. Blinky targets Pac-Man's own tile, Pinky targets four tiles ahead of him. The moment a ghost needs the true shortest route rather than a straight line estimate, it is a breadth first search on the tile grid, the panel at the top of this part.
vertices = walkable tiles · edges = adjacent tiles · BFS = distance from ghost

        
Roguelikes
Procedural dungeons
Games like The Binding of Isaac and Hades generate a new layout every run. The recursive backtracker above is the simplest way: DFS carves corridors and, because DFS visits every cell reachable from the start, the level is guaranteed to have a route from spawn to boss.
vertices = rooms · DFS = carve, backtrack when stuck · result = a spanning tree, so a maze with no loops

        
Social graphs
"People you may know"
Friend suggestions are BFS layer 2: the people two edges away from you, ranked by how many layer 1 vertices they share with you. The famous "six degrees" claim is a statement about BFS depth in a very large graph.
vertices = people · edges = friendships · layer k = distance k

        
The web
Crawlers
A crawler starts at a seed page, follows every link, and remembers what it has seen. That is BFS with a queue of URLs. The visited set is what stops it from crawling the same page forever, exactly as in Part 1.
vertices = pages · edges = links · queue = pages to fetch

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Part 03

      
Shortest paths: Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford, Floyd-Warshall, A*

      
BFS counts edges. Once edges have weights, a route with more edges can be cheaper, and BFS is wrong. All four algorithms here share one move, called relaxation: "I know a way to u costing d(u), and an edge u→v of weight w. If d(u) + w < d(v), I have just found a better way to v." They differ only in the order in which they relax.

Why it is allowed to settle a vertex for good

        When the cheapest vertex u leaves the queue, every other unsettled vertex is at least as far away. Any path that reaches u through one of them would cost at least that much plus a non negative edge, so nothing can beat d(u). This is the one place the proof uses w ≥ 0. Remove that assumption and the next panel shows the algorithm being confidently wrong.
        
O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap, since each vertex is popped once and each edge relaxed once

Why V - 1 rounds are enough, and what a V-th round means

        A shortest path has at most V - 1 edges. After round k every vertex whose shortest path uses k edges or fewer is correct, so after V - 1 rounds everything is. If a V-th round still improves something, no shortest path exists: there is a cycle whose total weight is negative and going round it again is always cheaper. That is what a currency arbitrage detector looks for.
        
O(V · E): V - 1 rounds, each relaxing every edge

Floyd-Warshall

      

        

          

          

            
            
            
          

          
Slide k to the right. At each step one more vertex is allowed as an intermediate stop, and every cell asks: is going through it cheaper than what I have?

Dynamic programming in one line

            D[i][j] = min( D[i][j], D[i][k] + D[k][j] ) for each k, then each i, then each j. Three nested loops over the vertices, so O(V³) time and a V × V table. Negative edges are fine. A negative number on the diagonal at the end means a negative cycle.

The one change

        Dijkstra pops the vertex with the smallest g(v), the cost so far. A* pops the smallest f(v) = g(v) + h(v), where h is a guess of the cost still to go, here the Manhattan distance to the goal. If h never overestimates, A* is still exact and simply wastes less effort in the wrong direction. Push the slider past 1 and h starts to overestimate: the search becomes greedy, faster still, and the path it returns may no longer be the shortest. Slide to 0 and A* is Dijkstra.

Where you have seen it

      

        
Navigation
GPS routing
Intersections are vertices, road segments are edges, weight is travel time. Live traffic simply rewrites the weights, and the same Dijkstra or A* run gives a new answer. The glowing escape route map in a heist film is this, redrawn every second as roads close.
weight = minutes · closed road = infinite weight · reroute = run again

        
Games
Unit pathfinding
Order fifty units across a map in a strategy game, or set a waypoint in an open world, and the engine runs A* on a navigation mesh, a graph whose vertices are walkable polygons. The heuristic is straight line distance, which never overestimates, so the path is exact and the search stays local.
vertices = navmesh polygons · h = straight line to target · A* = Dijkstra that looks where it is going

        
Networks
Packet routing
Routers running OSPF each hold a map of the network and run Dijkstra from themselves to fill their forwarding tables. The older distance vector protocols are Bellman-Ford spread out over many machines, each relaxing the edges it can see.
OSPF = Dijkstra per router · RIP = distributed Bellman-Ford

        
Finance
Arbitrage
Currencies are vertices, exchange rates are edges with weight -log(rate). A cycle of negative total weight is a sequence of trades that ends with more money than it started with. Bellman-Ford's final check is precisely the arbitrage detector.
negative cycle = free money, briefly

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Part 04

      
Minimum spanning trees: Kruskal and Prim

      
Part 1 defined a spanning tree: every vertex, no cycle, V - 1 edges. Add weights and ask for the cheapest one. Two greedy algorithms find it, and the surprise is that greed works here at all: taking the cheapest safe edge at every step is never a mistake.

The cut property, which is why greed is safe

        Split the vertices into any two groups. The cheapest edge crossing between the groups belongs to some minimum spanning tree. Kruskal uses this with the groups being "the two components this edge would join"; Prim uses it with the groups being "visited" and "not yet visited". Both are the same theorem wearing different clothes.

Where you have seen it

      

        
City builders
Power, water, roads
When a simulation game auto-wires new districts to the grid, the cheap and correct way is a minimum spanning tree on the districts with cable length as weight: every building powered, no redundant loop, least virtual money spent. The game above is exactly that problem at city scale.
vertices = districts · weight = metres of cable · MST = cheapest full coverage

        
Infrastructure
Telecom and utility networks
The original motivation. Borůvka published the first MST algorithm in 1926 to plan the electrification of Moravia. Fibre backbones, gas pipelines and rural electrification are still laid out this way, with a few extra edges added afterwards for redundancy.
MST = minimum to connect · extra edges = insurance

        
Sandbox games
Village paths and rail lines
Procedurally generated villages need every house reachable without wasting blocks. Compute an MST over house positions, then draw the tree edges as paths or minecart rail. No loops, everything connected, minimal material.
no cycles is a feature: one route between any two houses

        
Data
Clustering
Build the MST on points, then delete the k - 1 heaviest edges. What remains is k clusters, split at the widest gaps. This is single linkage clustering, and Kruskal is literally its inner loop.
cut the longest tree edges = separate the groups

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Part 05

      
Network flow and cuts

      
Now edges are pipes with a capacity. How much can travel from a source s to a sink t per unit of time? The answer comes with its own certificate: the bottleneck, a set of pipes whose total capacity equals the maximum flow. Cut them and nothing gets through.

The residual graph, and why paths can go backwards

        After pushing f units along an edge of capacity c, two things are possible: push up to c - f more forward, or undo up to f by sending flow backward. The residual graph has both edges. An augmenting path that uses a backward edge is rerouting flow already committed, which is how the algorithm escapes early bad choices. The search for a path is plain BFS on this residual graph; Edmonds-Karp is just the rule "always take the BFS path", which bounds the number of rounds and gives O(V E²).

The theorem

            Any flow is at most the capacity of any cut, since everything must cross it. Ford and Fulkerson proved the two meet: the maximum flow equals the minimum cut. When the algorithm stops because BFS finds no more path, the set of vertices BFS could still reach from s is one side of a minimum cut. You get the bottleneck for free.

Where you have seen it

      

        
Factory games
Conveyor throughput
In Factorio or Satisfactory every belt and pipe has a rate cap. The question "why does my output only reach 40 per minute?" is a max-flow question, and the answer is a min-cut: the set of belts that are saturated and together limit the whole line.
capacity = items per minute · saturated red edges = your bottleneck

        
Epidemics
Which hubs to close
Outbreak films show planners choosing airports to shut. Model flight routes as capacities and infected regions as the source; the minimum cut is the cheapest set of routes whose closure isolates the outbreak. The same model schedules evacuations and rations bandwidth.
source = outbreak · sink = protected region · min cut = routes to close

        
Heist films
Isolating the alarm
The hacker studying a wiring diagram to find "the three cables that cut the vault off from the control room without killing the lights" is choosing a minimum cut in a graph of wires, with the constraint that the rest stays connected.
vertices = junctions · cut = wires to sever · min = fewest wires

        
Scheduling
Crew assignment and bipartite problems
Many assignment problems reduce to flow: source to every crew member with capacity 1, crew to flights they can serve, flights to sink. Max flow is the largest number of flights that get a crew. Part 7's matching is this construction in disguise.
capacity 1 everywhere = each person used once

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Part 06

      
Topological sorting

      
A directed acyclic graph is a set of "this before that" rules. A topological order is a line-up that respects every rule: for every edge u → v, u comes first. Two algorithms produce one, and both double as a cycle detector, because a cycle is the one thing that makes such an order impossible.

Where you have seen it

      

        
Games
Skill trees and tech trees
Prerequisites in Path of Exile, Civilization or any RPG form a DAG. The engine sorts it once to know which unlocks are legal, and the in-degree table above is exactly the "you still need 2 more skills" counter shown on a locked node.
in-degree 0 = unlockable now · cycle = a tree nobody could ever complete

        
Tooling
Package managers and build systems
npm, pip, make and cargo all install or compile in topological order of the dependency graph. The dreaded "circular dependency" error is Kahn's queue running dry with vertices still unplaced.
vertices = packages · edge = depends on · install order = a topological order

        
Film and VFX
Render graphs
Houdini, Nuke and Maya model a shot as a node graph: geometry, simulation, lighting, compositing. The render farm sorts the graph so that the cloth simulation is computed before the lighting that depends on it, frame by frame. Video editors do the same for nested clips and effects.
levels from Kahn = nodes that can render in parallel

        
Spreadsheets
Which cell to recompute first
A spreadsheet is a DAG of formulas. Edit one cell and the program recomputes only its descendants, in topological order, so no cell is evaluated before its inputs. A circular reference is, again, a cycle.
edge = "this formula reads that cell"

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Part 07

      
Connectivity, matching and colouring

      
Four questions about the shape of a network rather than a route through it. Which vertices can all reach one another? Which single edge or vertex, if it failed, would split the network? Who should be paired with whom? And what is the fewest colours that keep neighbours different?

Why reversing the edges works, and the one pass alternative

        The vertex that finishes last in pass 1 belongs to a component that nothing else can reach into. Reverse every edge and start a DFS there: it can now reach only its own component, so the tree it grows is exactly one SCC. Pop the next unfinished vertex and repeat. Tarjan's algorithm gets the same result in a single DFS by keeping, for each vertex, the smallest discovery time reachable from its subtree, its low-link; a vertex whose low-link equals its own time is the root of a component. Both run in O(V + E).

Low-link again

            Run DFS and record each vertex's discovery time. For a tree edge u → v, ask whether v's subtree has any edge climbing back to u or above. If not, that is low(v) > time(u), then the edge is a bridge: cut it and the subtree floats away. If low(v) ≥ time(u) then u is an articulation point, the subtree can only get out through u. One DFS, O(V + E), both answers.

Augmenting paths, and what Hopcroft-Karp adds

        An augmenting path starts at an unmatched player, alternates unmatched edge, matched edge, unmatched edge, and ends at an unmatched role. Flip every edge on it and the matching grows by one. Berge's theorem says a matching is maximum exactly when no augmenting path exists, so "find one, flip it, repeat" is a complete algorithm: this is the O(V E) version above. Hopcroft-Karp finds a whole set of shortest disjoint augmenting paths per phase with a BFS layering, and only O(√V) phases are needed, giving O(E √V). It is the same flow idea as Part 5, with every capacity equal to 1.

Greedy is not optimal, but it is honest

        Finding the true minimum number of colours, the chromatic number, is NP-hard. Welsh-Powell is a heuristic: colour the busiest vertices first so that the colours they lock in are reused as widely as possible. It never uses more than max deg + 1 colours, and on the maps, timetables and register allocations it is used for it is usually within one of optimal. It runs in O(V²).
      

    


    

      
Where you have seen it

      

        
Competitive games
Matchmaking lobbies
Players waiting and open role slots form a bipartite graph, with an edge when a player's rating and role preference fit the slot. Filling a 5v5 is a maximum matching, and it must run in milliseconds for thousands of queued players, which is why Hopcroft-Karp and its flow based cousins exist.
left = players · right = slots · matching = a full lobby

        
Transit maps
Line colours
Two metro lines that share a station must not be drawn in the same colour, or the map is unreadable. That is graph colouring on the "shares a station" graph, and a puzzle like Mini Metro does it live as you draw lines.
vertices = lines · edge = shared station · colours = distinguishable on screen

        
Software
Dependency cycles
Run SCC on the import graph of a large codebase. Every component with more than one module is a cycle of imports, the thing that makes a module impossible to test or load on its own. Condense each SCC to one vertex and the result is a DAG, which Part 6 can sort.
SCC of size > 1 = circular imports · condensation = a DAG

        
Reliability
Where the network breaks
Bridges in a road network are literally bridges: a single road whose closure cuts a town off. Articulation points in a power grid or a data centre are the routers whose failure partitions everything. Finding them is the first step of any resilience audit.
bridge = one edge from disconnection · articulation point = one vertex
