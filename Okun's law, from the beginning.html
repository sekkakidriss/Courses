Okun's law

When an economy grows faster than usual, unemployment falls. When it grows slower, unemployment rises. In 1962 the economist Arthur Okun put a number on that link.

A textbook economy: unemployment starts at 5%, and the economy needs about 3% growth just to keep it there. Move the slider.

Two numbers

Before linking them, we need to know exactly what the two quantities are.

The idea

Okun's law connects two numbers that every statistics office publishes. The first is how much the economy produced, measured by GDP (Gross Domestic Product): the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. The second is the unemployment rate: the share of people who want a job and are looking for one, but do not have one.

The link between them is common sense. Producing things takes people. If firms want to produce more, they usually need more workers. If they produce less, some workers lose their jobs.

Notice that every person you remove from work lowers output and raises the unemployment rate at the same time. That is the seed of Okun's law. Real economies are messier, as we will see, but the direction is the same.

The formulas

Write \(E\) for the number employed, \(U\) for the number unemployed, and \(L = E + U\) for the labour force. The unemployment rate in percent is

\[ u = \frac{U}{L} \times 100. \]

Write \(Y_t\) for real GDP in year \(t\) ("real" means adjusted for price changes, so it measures quantities, not inflation). The growth rate of GDP in percent is

\[ g_t = \frac{Y_t - Y_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}} \times 100. \]

People who do not work and are not looking (students, retirees, people caring for family full time) are outside the labour force. They count in neither \(E\) nor \(U\).

Worked example

A city has 480,000 employed people and 20,000 unemployed people. The labour force is 500,000, so \(u = 20{,}000 / 500{,}000 \times 100 = 4\%\). Its real GDP went from 50.0 billion to 51.5 billion, so growth was \(1.5 / 50 \times 100 = 3\%\).

Check yourself

A labour force of 200 people includes 14 unemployed. What is the unemployment rate?
14 divided by 200 is 0.07, so 7%. The 93% would be the employment share of the labour force, not the unemployment rate.

Normal levels

Okun's law is not about output and unemployment directly. It is about how far each one sits from its normal level.

The idea

Even a healthy economy has some unemployment, because people change jobs, move cities, or finish their studies and start searching. Economists call this background level the natural rate of unemployment, written \(u^*\).

Output also has a normal level: what the economy can produce when its workers, machines and technology are used at a normal, sustainable intensity. This is called potential output, written \(Y^*\). It grows over time as the population grows and technology improves.

In a boom, actual output rises above potential and unemployment falls below its natural rate. In a recession, output falls below potential and unemployment rises above its natural rate. The two deviations move like mirror images.

The formulas

The output gap measures how far actual output is from potential, as a percentage of potential:

\[ \text{gap}_t = \frac{Y_t - Y^*_t}{Y^*_t} \times 100. \]

A negative gap means the economy is producing less than it could (a recession). The matching deviation for unemployment, \(u_t - u^*\), is called cyclical unemployment: the part of unemployment caused by the business cycle rather than by normal job changes.

Worked example

Potential output is 200 billion but actual output is 194 billion. The gap is \((194 - 200)/200 \times 100 = -3\%\). If the natural rate is 5% and actual unemployment is 6.5%, cyclical unemployment is 1.5 points.

One honest warning: \(Y^*\) and \(u^*\) cannot be observed directly. Statistics offices and central banks estimate them, usually with smoothing filters or models. We come back to this in block 7.

Check yourself

Actual output is 97 and potential output is 100. What is the output gap?
(97 - 100) / 100 x 100 = -3%. The economy is producing 3% less than its normal capacity.

The gap version

Okun's first formulation: a straight line between the output gap and cyclical unemployment.

The idea

Okun looked at United States data from the 1950s and noticed that the two deviations from block 2 lined up closely. When output was a few percent below potential, unemployment was above its normal level by roughly a third as many points. The relationship looked like a straight line through the origin: no gap, no cyclical unemployment.

The formula

\[ u_t - u^* = -\beta \, \text{gap}_t \]

The number \(\beta\) (beta) is Okun's coefficient. It answers one question: if output falls 1% below potential, by how many percentage points does unemployment rise above its natural rate? The minus sign says the two move in opposite directions. Okun's own estimate was about \(\beta \approx 0.3\). Many modern estimates for the United States are closer to 0.5.

Try it

Raise \(\beta\) and the line gets steeper: the same recession throws more people out of work. Lower it and unemployment barely reacts.

Reading it backwards

The same equation can be read as a cost. Divide both sides by \(-\beta\): each point of cyclical unemployment goes with a gap of \(1/\beta\) percent of output. With \(\beta = 0.5\), one extra point of unemployment means roughly 2% of GDP not produced. Okun's original figure was about 3%, which is why his result is often quoted as "1 point of unemployment costs 3% of output".

Worked example

A country has a natural rate of 4.5%, an output gap of -2%, and \(\beta = 0.4\). Then \(u = 4.5 - 0.4 \times (-2) = 4.5 + 0.8 = 5.3\%\).

Check yourself

With \(\beta = 0.5\) and \(u^* = 5\%\), unemployment is 8%. What output gap does Okun's law imply?
Cyclical unemployment is 8 - 5 = 3 points. Then 3 = -0.5 x gap, so gap = -6%.

The growth version

The form most often used in practice, because it needs only growth rates, which are published every quarter.

The idea

The gap version needs potential output, which nobody observes. Okun also proposed a version that avoids it. Think about what makes the gap change from one year to the next. Potential output grows at some steady rate, call it \(g^*\). If actual output grows faster than that, the economy catches up with its potential and the gap closes, so unemployment falls. If it grows slower, the gap widens and unemployment rises.

So the rule becomes: unemployment stays put when the economy grows at its normal pace \(g^*\), and every point of growth above or below that pace moves unemployment by \(\beta\) points in the opposite direction.

From the gap version to the growth version

Write the gap version for this year and for last year, then subtract. The natural rate cancels:

\[ (u_t - u^*) - (u_{t-1} - u^*) = -\beta \,(\text{gap}_t - \text{gap}_{t-1}). \]

The left side is simply the change in unemployment, \(\Delta u_t = u_t - u_{t-1}\). On the right, the gap changes by approximately actual growth minus potential growth, \(\text{gap}_t - \text{gap}_{t-1} \approx g_t - g^*\). Putting these together:

\[ \Delta u_t = -\beta\,(g_t - g^*). \]

The value \(g^*\) is sometimes called the break-even growth rate: the growth needed just to keep unemployment from rising.

Try it

Set the growth rate for five years. The economy starts at 5% unemployment, with \(\beta = 0.5\) and \(g^* = 3\%\).

Try "Slow growth": the economy never shrinks, yet unemployment keeps rising. Positive growth is not enough. It has to beat \(g^*\).

Worked example

With \(\beta = 0.5\) and \(g^* = 3\%\), suppose the economy grows by only 1%. Then \(\Delta u = -0.5 \times (1 - 3) = +1\) point. Unemployment rises from 5% to 6% even though output grew.

Check yourself

With \(\beta = 0.4\) and \(g^* = 2.5\%\), what growth rate lowers unemployment by 1 point in a year?
We need -1 = -0.4 x (g - 2.5), so g - 2.5 = 2.5 and g = 5%.

Estimating it

Where the numbers \(\beta\) and \(g^*\) come from: a straight line fitted through past data.

The idea

Take a country's history: for each year, the GDP growth rate and the change in unemployment. Plot one dot per year. If Okun's law holds, the dots fall roughly along a downward-sloping line. The slope of that line tells us \(\beta\), and where it crosses zero tells us \(g^*\).

The formula

We fit the line \(\Delta u_t = a + b\,g_t + \varepsilon_t\), where \(\varepsilon_t\) is everything the line does not explain. Comparing with block 4, \(\Delta u_t = \beta g^* - \beta g_t\), so

\[ \beta = -b, \qquad g^* = -\frac{a}{b}. \]

The standard way to choose \(a\) and \(b\) is OLS (Ordinary Least Squares): pick the line that makes the sum of squared vertical distances from the dots to the line as small as possible. The solution has a closed form:

\[ \hat b = \frac{\sum_t (g_t - \bar g)(\Delta u_t - \overline{\Delta u})}{\sum_t (g_t - \bar g)^2}, \qquad \hat a = \overline{\Delta u} - \hat b\,\bar g. \]

Worked example

A regression on annual data gives \(\Delta u = 1.2 - 0.4\,g\). Then \(\beta = 0.4\) and \(g^* = 1.2 / 0.4 = 3\%\). In words: unemployment is stable at 3% growth, and each extra point of growth lowers it by 0.4 points.

With real data, analysts usually check that the estimate is stable across sub-periods, and many use quarterly data with a few lags of growth, because unemployment tends to react to output with some delay.

Check yourself

A fitted line is \(\Delta u = 0.9 - 0.3\,g\). What are \(\beta\) and \(g^*\)?
\(\beta\) is minus the slope, so 0.3. Break-even growth is 0.9 / 0.3 = 3%.

Why not one for one

If output falls 4%, why does unemployment rise by only about 2 points, not 4?

The idea

On our island in block 1, output and jobs moved exactly together. Real economies have shock absorbers. When demand falls, a firm has several options before firing anyone:

ChannelWhat happens in a downturn
ProductivityFirms keep workers even when there is less to do, because hiring and training replacements later is expensive. This is called labour hoarding. Output per hour falls.
HoursWorkers stay employed but work fewer hours: less overtime, shorter shifts, part-time arrangements.
ParticipationSome people who lose jobs stop searching (discouraged workers) or never start. They leave the labour force, so they are not counted as unemployed.
UnemploymentWhatever is left shows up as people losing jobs while still looking for work.

Only the last channel moves the unemployment rate. Okun's coefficient is, roughly, the share of an output swing that ends up in that channel.

The formula

Output can be split into a product of pieces: output per hour, hours per worker, the employment rate, the participation rate, and the working-age population \(N\):

\[ Y = \frac{Y}{H}\cdot\frac{H}{E}\cdot\frac{E}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{N}\cdot N. \]

Growth rates of a product add up approximately, so a percentage change in \(Y\) is the sum of the percentage changes of the pieces. The employment rate is \(E/L = 1 - u/100\), so a 1% fall in it means unemployment rises by about 1 point.

Try it

A recession pushes output below its normal path. Decide how much of the hit each shock absorber takes. Whatever you do not assign falls on unemployment.

Worked example

Output falls 2% below normal. Productivity absorbs 0.8 points, hours 0.4, participation 0.2. That leaves \(2 - 0.8 - 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.6\) points for the employment rate, so unemployment rises about 0.6 points. The implied coefficient is \(0.6 / 2 = 0.3\), close to what Okun found.

Check yourself

A country introduces a scheme that pays firms to cut hours instead of firing workers. What happens to its Okun coefficient?
More of each output fall is absorbed by hours, so less reaches unemployment. The coefficient gets smaller. Germany's short-time work scheme in 2009 is the classic example.

Around the world

The coefficient is not a constant of nature. It depends on how a country's labour market works.

The idea

Because \(\beta\) measures how much of an output swing reaches unemployment, countries with flexible hiring and firing have large coefficients, and countries where firms protect jobs have small ones. The values below are rounded illustrations of a pattern found in published cross-country studies. Exact estimates vary by study, method and period, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

Where it breaks down

IssueWhat it means for the rule
It is a regularity, not a lawThe name is historical. It is a pattern in the data, and the coefficient drifts as institutions change.
Jobless recoveriesAfter some recessions (United States 1991, 2001, 2009) output recovered well before jobs did, so unemployment stayed high longer than the rule predicted.
Job retention schemesIn 2020, countries with furlough schemes saw output collapse while unemployment barely moved. In the United States, with fewer such schemes, unemployment jumped far more.
Unobserved quantitiesPotential output and the natural rate are estimates and get revised, which changes the gap version's inputs after the fact.
Data revisionsEarly GDP figures are revised for years. A fit on first-release data can differ from a fit on final data.
AsymmetryUnemployment often rises faster in recessions than it falls in expansions, which a single straight line cannot capture.

Check yourself

Two countries suffer the same 3% output gap. Country A has \(\beta = 0.8\), country B has \(\beta = 0.2\). How much more does unemployment rise in A?
A: 0.8 x 3 = 2.4 points. B: 0.2 x 3 = 0.6 points. The difference is 1.8 points (A rises 4 times as much, but that is 2.4 versus 0.6, not 3 more).

Putting it to work

The two everyday uses: turning a GDP forecast into an unemployment forecast, and the reverse.

The idea

Forecasters often predict GDP growth first, because it comes out of their main macroeconomic models. Okun's law then gives a quick, transparent translation into unemployment. Run backwards, it answers a policy question: how fast must the economy grow to bring unemployment down to a target?

\[ \text{Forecast:}\quad u_{t+1} = u_t - \beta\,(g_{t+1} - g^*) \qquad \text{Target:}\quad g_{\text{needed}} = g^* - \frac{u_{\text{target}} - u_t}{\beta} \]

Try it

Worked example

A central bank expects 1.5% growth next year. Unemployment is 6%, \(\beta = 0.5\) and \(g^* = 3\%\). The forecast is \(6 - 0.5 \times (1.5 - 3) = 6.75\%\). To bring unemployment down to 5% instead, the economy would need \(3 - (5 - 6)/0.5 = 5\%\) growth.

Check yourself

With \(g^* = 2\%\) and \(\beta = 0.25\), unemployment is 7% and the target is 6.5% next year. What growth is needed?
g = 2 - (6.5 - 7)/0.25 = 2 + 2 = 4%.
